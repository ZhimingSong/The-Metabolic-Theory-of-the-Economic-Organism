Part I: Viewing the Economy Through a Different Lens


The Metabolic Theory of the Economic Organism：Chapter 3: The Anatomy of the Economic Organism: Structures, Organizations, and Nested Hierarchies

Abstract

This paper is Chapter 3 of the book The Metabolic Theory of the Economic Organism: From the Mechanistic Paradigm to the Living Systems. The complete manuscript of the book is available on SSRN (DOI: 10.2139/ssrn.6456938) and OSF Preprints (DOI: 10.17605/OSF.IO/KPXNY).

This chapter develops the anatomical foundation of the Economic Organism–Metabolism Theory (EOMT) through a systematic structural map across nested levels. All functional correspondences are established under two criteria—functional irreplaceability and correspondence of failure modes—ensuring diagnostic rather than merely illustrative value.

At the cellular level, individuals act as behavioral carriers embedded within organizations. Households function as reproductive and consumption units, while firms serve as primary productive cells whose boundaries reflect the trade-off between internal coordination and market exchange. Large-scale firm failure generates cascading dysfunction analogous to tissue-level breakdown.

At the tissue level, industries are conceptualized as collections of firms rather than one-to-one organ analogues, correcting a common analytical error. This perspective captures firm aggregation, cross-industry mobility, and shifting functional dominance across development stages. Three laws of industrial metabolism are identified: differentiation, waste accumulation, and renewal. Crisis emerges when waste exceeds excretory capacity or when firm exit is obstructed, as in the case of zombie firms. Markets function as inter-tissue coordination interfaces, whose failures—monopoly, information asymmetry, and price distortion—correspond to breakdowns in coordination.

At the regulatory level, a distinction is drawn between money as circulatory medium and policy as regulatory signaling. Financial crises are interpreted as disruptions in circulation or signal transmission, including credit freezes as circulatory arrest and liquidity traps as receptor failure. Under such conditions, fiscal intervention becomes necessary. The price mechanism operates as a distributed coordination system, while government serves as a central regulatory authority when decentralized coordination fails. A further epigenetic layer—culture and historically embedded norms—imposes path-dependent constraints beyond the reach of short-run policy.

At the systemic level, the chapter develops a holonic nested structure in which each unit is both whole and part of a larger system. It traces the historical emergence of the global economic super-organism from loosely connected regional systems to deeply integrated structures, highlighting its contingent and reversible nature. Recent deglobalization trends are interpreted as adaptive responses to declining system-level stability.

The chapter concludes with a unified anatomical framework and a diagnostic principle: structural problems require interventions at corresponding levels, and mismatched responses risk masking deeper systemic imbalances.


Keywords: economic anatomy; metabolic organism; holonic structure; firms as cells; industries as tissues; monetary system; monetary policy hormones; global super-organism; historical coupling



The central question of this chapter is the following: if the economy is an organism, what does its bodily structure look like? What physiological roles are played by firms, industries, markets, governments, and money? And when we speak of an “economic organism,” which level of living system are we actually referring to?


In the previous two chapters, we established the life-system paradigm for analyzing economic systems and carried out a first redefinition of value at the theoretical level. Yet a theory that contains only a philosophical orientation and a definition of value, but lacks structural description, cannot truly enter the realm of analysis.

Before diagnosing disease, physicians must first master anatomy—they must understand the functions, locations, and relationships of every tissue and organ. Likewise, if economists are to genuinely apply the life-system paradigm, they require an “anatomical map of the economic organism”: they must know which structures perform which functions, which levels of pathology produce which symptoms, and at what scale particular problems must be addressed.

This chapter provides precisely such an anatomical map. But before drawing it, the methodological status of this map must first be clarified.

3.1 The Premise of Economic Anatomy: Criteria for Functional Correspondence

In biology, there exists a profound correspondence between structure and function. The pumping function of the heart derives from the specific geometric structure of its muscular chambers. Neurons are able to transmit electrical signals because of the arrangement of ion channels within their cell membranes. Immune cells can recognize invading agents because of the precise spatial configuration of receptors on their surfaces. Structure is not merely the outer shell of function; it is the physical basis of function itself.

This principle also applies to economic systems. Each type of structure within the economic organism performs a specific function not because it was designed by some external planner, but because, through long historical evolution, stable correspondences have emerged between particular structures and particular functions. Structures that perform indispensable functions tend to be preserved and strengthened, while those that are functionally redundant or poorly adapted are gradually eliminated or marginalized.

However, unlike biological anatomy, economic anatomy faces a particular epistemological challenge. Correspondences cannot be established solely on the basis of intuitive similarity, otherwise the analysis would degenerate into arbitrary rhetoric. One scholar might compare the financial sector to a “circulatory system,” while another might compare it to a “digestive system.” Both comparisons could appear plausible, yet neither would be testable against the other.

For this reason, the present book adopts two criteria when establishing each functional correspondence.

Criterion One: Functional Irreplaceability

If the removal of a particular economic structure produces a specific and predictable metabolic disorder within the system, and this disorder cannot be compensated for by other structures, then the correspondence between that structure and its physiological function can be considered valid.

For example, if firms—as independent decision-making production organizations—were removed, dispersed individual labor would not spontaneously organize itself into coordinated large-scale production. This logic mirrors the irreplaceability of cells within biological systems.

Criterion Two: Correspondence of Failure Modes

If a pathological failure within an economic structure produces symptoms whose logical structure corresponds to the biological symptoms produced by failure in the analogous physiological structure, then the correspondence possesses analytical value at the level of diagnosis.

For instance, the collapse of a financial system—when liquidity completely ceases—generates economic symptoms that closely resemble those produced by the complete shutdown of a circulatory system in biological organisms. In both cases, the simultaneous interruption of vital flows leads to systemic breakdown: capital supply across industries is cut off just as blood supply to organs is interrupted, resulting in functional failure.

3.2 Cellular Level: Individuals, Firms, and Households

Any complex organism is composed of cells. Cells are the smallest functional units of life: they can independently perform basic metabolic processes (absorbing energy, synthesizing substances, and expelling waste), respond to environmental signals, divide and proliferate, and eventually age and die. A cell does not need to understand the operational logic of the entire organism. It only needs to complete local metabolic processes within its immediate microenvironment, while the overall function of the organism emerges from the coordinated activity of countless cells.

Economic systems exhibit a highly similar hierarchical structure. Macroeconomic order is not directly designed by a single central agent; rather, it gradually emerges from the local decisions and interactions of a large number of decentralized units. To understand such a system, one must first identify its basic functional units.

Within the economic organism, this role is primarily performed by two types of structures: households and firms. They respectively undertake reproductive metabolism and productive metabolism, forming two fundamental types of cells that sustain the continuous operation of the economic system.

Before proceeding further, however, it is necessary to clarify a frequently overlooked question: what position do individuals occupy within this structure?

3.2.1 Individuals: The Basic Carriers of Metabolic Behavior

In mainstream economics, individuals are typically treated as the smallest unit of analysis, and their behavior is described through the “rational agent” assumption. Within the analytical framework of the life-system analogy, however, individuals are more appropriately understood as carriers of behavior within the economic metabolic process, rather than as the fundamental structural units of the entire system.

In biological organisms, although the cell is the basic unit of life, the interior of each cell contains complex functional structures—including enzyme systems, mitochondria, and various signaling molecules. These structures perform specific tasks such as energy transformation, material synthesis, and information transmission. Yet none of them, by themselves, constitute an independent living entity. Only when these functional components are organized within the boundary of a cell and coordinated through a unified metabolic logic can stable life processes be sustained.

Individuals in economic systems display a certain functional similarity to these internal cellular components. Individuals perform micro-level functions such as perception, decision-making, and execution. They perceive price signals and environmental changes, make decisions regarding consumption, labor, or investment, and participate in production and exchange through concrete actions. In most cases, however, these behaviors do not occur in complete isolation but are embedded within organizational structures such as firms and households. Firms provide stable boundaries for resource allocation in production, while households organize labor reproduction and consumption decisions.

From this perspective, individuals embedded within organizational structures can, at the functional level, be compared to the execution units within a cell that carry out specific metabolic tasks. They respond to local environmental signals and collectively sustain the operation of the overall metabolic process through numerous micro-level reactions, without needing to comprehend the operational logic of the entire system. Macroeconomic order gradually emerges from this multitude of localized decisions and interactions. In this respect, the mechanism resembles the “decentralized knowledge coordination” emphasized by Friedrich Hayek: systemic order does not depend on the comprehensive rationality of any single actor but emerges through the interaction of localized information and localized decision-making.

It should be noted that not all individuals remain stably embedded within organizational structures such as firms or households. In real economies, there are also individuals with relatively low degrees of metabolic embeddedness, including the temporarily unemployed, beggars, migrant populations, or even individuals who choose materially self-sufficient lifestyles. Such individuals may partially detach from typical production or consumption structures for certain periods, but this does not imply that they exist entirely outside the economic system.

Analogous situations also exist in biological systems. Certain immune cells, for example, move between different tissues while performing surveillance and defensive functions, and some signaling molecules diffuse between cells to regulate the overall metabolic state. For periods of time, they do not belong permanently to a specific cellular structure, yet they remain within the metabolic network of the entire organism.

From a functional-structural perspective, such mobile individuals can be understood as behavioral carriers with relatively low levels of embeddedness within the economic system. They may temporarily detach from typical production or consumption organizations, but they still maintain connections with the broader economic network in various ways. Even seemingly self-sufficient lifestyles often depend on existing technological systems, accumulated tools, or broader social infrastructure.

Therefore, within the economic organism, the relationship between individuals and organizational structures is better understood as a continuously varying degree of metabolic embeddedness. Most individuals are embedded in the system's fundamental metabolic structures through households and firms, while a small number of marginal individuals can be regarded as behavioral nodes with lower degrees of embeddedness, though they still remain within the overall metabolic network of the economy.

From this perspective, the “rational agent” assumption in traditional economics can be interpreted as a simplified description. Individuals do not need to possess complete rationality or perfect information; they only need to respond adaptively to local environmental signals. Such boundedly rational behavioral patterns bear a certain functional resemblance to the way cells respond to local signals within biological systems.

3.2.2 Households: Cells of Reproduction and Consumption

Within the economic organism, households can be understood as the fundamental cells responsible for reproductive and consumption metabolism.

First, households perform the function of labor reproduction. Through processes such as childrearing, education, and socialization, households cultivate new individuals capable of participating in economic activity, thereby continuously supplying the system with new behavioral carriers. From a long-term perspective, this function constitutes an important source of the population base of the economic system.

Second, households undertake the function of consumption metabolism. Households absorb goods and services produced by firms and transform them into various activities that sustain life and improve welfare, thereby completing a crucial link in the economic metabolic cycle. Consumption is not merely the simple depletion of resources; it also forms the demand foundation that allows the production system to continue operating.

Households also perform a certain degree of risk-buffering function. When income fluctuates or economic uncertainty rises, households often maintain basic living standards through savings, internal resource reallocation, and mutual support among members. In this way, they help stabilize the overall rhythm of economic metabolism.

From a historical perspective, households in many societies have also performed production functions simultaneously. In agricultural societies or traditional handicraft economies, many households served as both production and consumption units, with production activities largely occurring within the household itself. From a metabolic-structural perspective, such households can be understood as integrated cells with relatively low degrees of functional differentiation, simultaneously containing both production and reproductive processes.

As social division of labor deepened, production activities gradually separated from the household structure and acquired more stable structural boundaries within new organizational forms. This process created the conditions for the emergence of firms as specialized production cells.

From the perspective of systemic failure modes, dysfunction at the household level often produces deep and persistent effects. For example, a prolonged contraction in consumption can be interpreted as a weakening of consumption metabolism; a sustained decline in fertility indicates the deterioration of reproductive mechanisms, thereby weakening the long-term demographic foundation of the system; and abnormally high savings rates may indicate that households are operating in a defensive risk posture and lack confidence in the broader metabolic environment. Structurally, such phenomena resemble the protective contraction initiated by cells when they perceive metabolic threats. Their underlying causes often lie in broader institutional and environmental structures rather than in the behavioral choices of individual households.

3.2.3 Firms: Differentiated Cells of Production

With the development of social division of labor and market exchange systems, production activities gradually separated from the household structure and acquired more stable metabolic boundaries within the organizational form of the firm. In modern economic systems, firms therefore become the principal cellular structures responsible for productive metabolism.

The correspondence between firms and cells can be examined through two criteria.

First, functional irreplaceability. If the firm as an independent decision-making boundary were removed and complex production activities were reduced either to individual labor or to centralized state directives, coordinated production under conditions of large-scale division of labor would become difficult to achieve. Through their internal organizational structures, firms integrate dispersed individual behaviors into stable production processes, thereby forming productive metabolic units with a certain degree of adaptive capacity.

Second, correspondence of failure modes. When large numbers of firms simultaneously go bankrupt, the economic system typically experiences cascading effects such as surging unemployment, supply-chain disruptions, and investment stagnation. At the level of systemic structure, such symptoms exhibit a functional isomorphism with the decline of tissue function caused by large-scale cellular death in biological organisms.

The correspondence between firms and cells is also reflected in a key characteristic: selective boundaries. The cell membrane functions as a selective barrier between the cell and its external environment, determining which substances may enter and which must be expelled. Firms likewise possess organizational boundaries. Within the boundaries defined by property rights and organizational structure, firms can autonomously allocate resources and coordinate internal behavior. Outside these boundaries, however, exchanges must occur through market mechanisms with other economic units.

Ronald Coase, in his discussion of the nature of the firm, argued that the existence of firm boundaries originates from differences in transaction costs: when the costs of internal organization are lower than the costs of market transactions, the boundary of the firm expands outward. From a metabolic perspective, this conclusion can be interpreted as reflecting efficiency differences between productive metabolic units and their external metabolic environment. The position of the firm's boundary therefore depends, to some extent, on the relative efficiency of internal organizational coordination and external market exchange.

Oliver Williamson's analysis of asset specificity further illustrates this point. When certain production activities depend heavily on particular organizational relationships, they tend to be integrated within the same organizational boundary in order to maintain stable production structures.

Cells form the foundation of living systems, yet the simple aggregation of cells does not automatically produce a complete organism. Cells must organize into tissues with differentiated functions at higher levels and coordinate through complex mechanisms to maintain the stability of the overall structure. The same holds for economic systems: households and firms are merely the foundational cells. Only through further functional differentiation and structural integration can they form what may properly be called a true economic organism.

3.3 Functional Differentiation: Cellular Tissues — The Metabolic Logic of Industries

In biological organisms, cells differentiate into tissues with distinct functions: muscle tissue, nervous tissue, epithelial tissue, and connective tissue. A tissue is not a simple aggregation of cells; rather, it is a functional unit formed when similar or related cells assemble according to a specific functional logic. Tissues possess three key characteristics. First, cells within a tissue share similar metabolic characteristics and divisions of labor. Second, cells can move between tissues (cellular replacement), enabling tissues to renew themselves. Third, tissues constitute the material from which organs are formed—organs are composed of multiple tissues and perform higher-level integrative functions.

Industries, in the economic organism, correspond to cellular tissues, rather than organs.

This positioning is intentional, as it corrects a common analytical error: directly mapping industries onto specific biological organs (for example, “agriculture = digestive system” or “finance = circulatory system”) and constructing a forced one-to-one correspondence. Such mappings contain a fundamental flaw. They require each industry to correspond to a single physiological function, whereas in reality most industries simultaneously participate in multiple metabolic processes. More importantly, the functions of organs are determined by their physical structure and are therefore relatively fixed, while the functional boundaries of industries are historically evolved and dynamically changing.

Understanding industries as cellular tissues therefore provides a more accurate analytical logic.

First, industries contain economic cells (firms and households), just as tissues contain cells. An industry is not a single firm but rather a collection of numerous firms operating under similar metabolic logics. Manufacturing industries encompass firms engaged in material transformation; financial industries encompass firms engaged in capital intermediation; service industries encompass various firms engaged in information processing and relational maintenance. Industry-level analysis focuses on the overall metabolic health of this class of cells rather than on the fate of any individual firm.

Second, just as cells can move between tissues, economic cells can also move between industries. In biological organisms, certain cells possess the ability to migrate across tissues—stem cells can differentiate into different tissues, and immune cells can circulate among them. In economic systems, labor and capital can flow from one industry to another, and firms can cross industry boundaries through diversification. This cross-industry mobility of factors constitutes a crucial mechanism for the self-renewal of the economic system. When a particular industry contracts, its cells (firms and labor) migrate toward other industries, thereby preventing systemic necrosis; when an industry expands rapidly, it attracts cells from other industries, achieving rapid growth at the organizational level.

One core function of industrial policy is precisely to guide this process of cellular replacement—reducing frictions in cross-industry mobility (such as through skill retraining or industrial subsidies), or preventing certain industry organizations from excessively attracting cells and thereby generating metabolic imbalance (for example, the “Dutch disease” phenomenon in which the financial sector in some economies attracts an excessive share of talent).

Third, the functional position of industries changes across metabolic stages. This is precisely where the tissue analogy is superior to the organ analogy. In the early stages of industrialization, manufacturing is the central metabolic tissue, performing the primary function of transforming material energy into ordered structures. In the digital economy, information technology and service industries increasingly assume the role of creating ordered structures. Such historical shifts in functional dominance are difficult to explain under an organ analogy (since organ functions are relatively stable), but they are entirely natural under the tissue analogy: different types of tissues assume dominant metabolic roles at different stages of development. Biology provides clear parallels—for example, during embryonic development the functional dominance of different tissues evolves across developmental stages.

3.3.1 Three General Laws of Industrial Metabolism

Based on the analytical framework of cellular tissues, industrial metabolism exhibits three patterns that can be observed across different economies.

The law of metabolic organizational differentiation.

As the economic organism becomes more complex, industries continuously differentiate—from early agriculture and handicrafts, to the multiple branches of manufacturing during industrialization, to modern highly specialized metabolic organizations such as professional services, digital platforms, and biotechnology. This differentiation mirrors the logic of tissue differentiation during biological development: increasing complexity is a necessary condition for rising levels of order, and the increase in order constitutes the physical foundation of value creation (as discussed in Chapter 2).

The law of metabolic waste accumulation.

Each type of industrial organization produces specific forms of metabolic waste while performing its metabolic functions. Industrial organizations generate material waste (pollution, carbon emissions); financial organizations generate value waste (bad debts, zombie liabilities); informational organizations generate informational waste (data noise, attention pollution). When the accumulation of waste exceeds the system's capacity for elimination, metabolic crises emerge at the industry level. This principle will be elaborated in detail in Chapter 5 (The Theory of Metabolic Waste).

The law of metabolic replacement and renewal.

Continuous cellular replacement occurs within industries—inefficient firms exit, efficient firms expand, and new firms enter. This mechanism is essential for maintaining metabolic vitality within industries. When replacement mechanisms are obstructed (for instance, when “zombie firms” are artificially sustained by policy), metabolic waste accumulates more rapidly and the overall metabolic efficiency of the industry declines. One of the reasons behind Japan's “Lost Thirty Years” was precisely the large-scale persistence of zombie firms, which obstructed cellular replacement within manufacturing organizations and prevented industries from completing their metabolic renewal.

3.3.2 The Service Sector: An Underestimated Metabolic Organization

In traditional economic analysis, the service sector is often viewed as a derivative of manufacturing—something that expands only after societies become sufficiently wealthy and shift consumption from material goods to services. This perspective underestimates the metabolic function of the service sector within the economic organism.

In advanced economies, services typically account for 70–80 percent of GDP, making them the largest category of metabolic organizations. Within the negentropy framework, the metabolic function of services follows a distinctive logic: they primarily engage in the creation of meaning value and informational value, rather than the transformation of material value.

The education sector establishes ordered structures at the cognitive level (transmitting knowledge from one generation to the next represents an intergenerational transfer of informational negentropy). The healthcare sector maintains the metabolic health of biological organisms (serving as the material maintenance system for economic cells—households and labor). Retail and logistics constitute the metabolic circulation network (the capillary structure that transports material value from production to consumption). Professional services such as consulting, law, and accounting act as localized providers of institutional and informational order.

The anatomical significance of the service sector within the economic organism lies in the fact that it represents the principal carrier of metabolic decoupling. As the share of services increases, economic growth becomes increasingly dependent on the creation of negentropy at the informational and symbolic levels rather than on the expansion of material energy consumption. This represents the industry-level manifestation of the “metabolic lightening” trend described in Chapter 2.

3.3.3 Markets: Coordinating Interfaces Between Industrial Organizations

If industries are functionally differentiated cellular tissues, then markets constitute the interface systems through which metabolic substances and information are exchanged between different tissues.

In biological organisms, exchanges between tissues occur through various interfaces. The extracellular matrix provides the physical environment for material support and signal transmission; capillary networks penetrate each tissue to facilitate material exchange; nerve endings transmit regulatory signals to every node at the tissue level. A common characteristic of these interfaces is that they are not independent organs but functional structures embedded between tissues that make coordination possible.

Markets play precisely this role within the economic organism. Markets are neither independent industries nor organs that can be analyzed in isolation; rather, they are the interfaces through which value exchanges occur between industries and firms. Price signals are generated, transmitted, and received on these interfaces, enabling dispersed metabolic decisions to coordinate with one another.

The most common misunderstanding about markets is that they are often discussed as a “mechanism” or “tool,” as if markets were simply a set of rules that could be designed, adjusted, or replaced at will. From the perspective of the life paradigm, however, markets are better understood as evolutionarily formed coordination interface systems. They emerge historically from countless localized interactions among micro-level actors and, once formed, acquire their own physiological logic that cannot easily be replaced or redesigned.

Friedrich Hayek's argument that markets function as a “mechanism for coordinating dispersed knowledge” gains a more precise expression within the metabolic perspective: the market interface allows industrial organizations to coordinate their metabolic decisions through local price signals without needing to understand the overall metabolic state of the system. This corresponds to the physiological logic through which cells participate in the coordination of the entire organism while sensing only local signals.

The core function of the market interface is the generation and transmission of price signals. Prices are real-time metabolic signals through which information about scarcity and value—distributed across millions of decentralized decisions—is aggregated, compressed, and broadcast throughout the economic system by the market interface. The quality of this signal—its accuracy, timeliness, and credibility—directly determines the overall metabolic efficiency of the economic organism.

When the market interface is impaired, price signals become distorted and metabolic coordination becomes disordered. Monopoly removes the competitive benchmark for prices, systematically distorting signals. Severe information asymmetry (as described by George Akerlof's “market for lemons”) causes buyers and sellers to receive asynchronous signals, producing communication breakdowns at the interface. Excessive administrative price intervention may inject artificially set prices into market interfaces where signals should emerge spontaneously—much like introducing incorrect chemical signals that disrupt normal communication between tissues, thereby generating systemic metabolic disorder.

It is important to note that not all market interfaces possess equal metabolic importance. Factor markets (labor, capital, and land markets) constitute the fundamental interfaces for cellular replacement across industries; their failure obstructs cross-industry metabolic flows. Product and service markets serve as the interface for the realization of final value; their failure leads to metabolic disconnection between production and consumption. Financial markets serve as the interface for the intertemporal allocation of metabolic resources; their failure—such as the sudden freezing of credit markets in 2008—can trigger a liquidity crisis throughout the entire metabolic system. The metabolic importance of different market interfaces determines the priority of their regulation: the more fundamental the interface to systemic metabolism, the stronger the systemic transmission effects of its failure, and therefore the greater the need for institutional maintenance and protection.

3.4 Regulatory Systems: Neural, Circulatory, Endocrine, and Immune Systems

With cells, cellular tissues, and coordinating interfaces in place, a regulatory system is still required for a structure to become a true organism. Biological organisms possess three principal regulatory systems: the nervous system (rapid electrical signal transmission with millisecond-level responses), the circulatory and endocrine systems (which transport substances and hormones through the bloodstream, operating on timescales from seconds to hours), and the immune system (which identifies and eliminates harmful agents, responding over days to weeks). These three systems differ in speed, scope of action, and duration, yet they operate in coordination to maintain the organism's metabolic homeostasis.

The economic organism likewise possesses three corresponding regulatory mechanisms. Before establishing these correspondences, they must again be examined using the two criteria defined in this chapter: functional irreplaceability and correspondence of failure modes.

3.4.1 Neural Networks: Price Signals and Information Systems

The core function of the nervous system is to transmit signals at extremely high speed within the organism, thereby coordinating the behavior of tissues and cells. The neural structure of biological organisms is not composed of a single level but instead contains multiple layers. On the one hand, there exist highly automated peripheral and autonomic nervous systems, which rapidly trigger responses to local environmental changes. On the other hand, there exists a higher-level central nervous system that integrates information and, when necessary, issues system-wide commands.

The economic organism exhibits a similar structure. First, there exists a highly decentralized and automatically operating neural network layer, composed of the price mechanism, information transmission channels, and systems of market expectations. This network performs the signal-transmission function within the economic system most of the time.

Price signals within this network play a role analogous to sensory nerve signals. They continuously aggregate and transmit information dispersed across countless micro-level decisions—changes in consumer preferences, fluctuations in production costs, and variations in resource scarcity—throughout the system. A change in price is effectively a signal pulse that prompts economic actors to adjust production, consumption, and investment behavior.

The market price mechanism emphasized by Friedrich Hayek represents a typical manifestation of such a decentralized neural network. No single actor possesses all information, yet the rapid transmission of price signals allows numerous localized decisions to generate a coordinated order at the systemic level.

In modern economies, this neural network includes more complex information transmission structures. Media organizations, analytical institutions, rating agencies, and credit systems form mechanisms that interpret and amplify price signals. They analyze, filter, and retransmit original signals, producing the narratives and expectation structures widely observed in markets. These narratives themselves subsequently influence economic behavior, thereby further altering the transmission pathways of price signals.

The economic neural network, therefore, is not merely a single price mechanism but rather a distributed signal system composed of prices, information, and expectations.

The health of this system is crucial for the coordination capacity of the economic organism. When the neural network experiences dysfunction—for example, severe information distortion, large-scale rumor propagation, or uncontrolled market sentiment—the economic system may exhibit symptoms analogous to “neurological disorders”: herd behavior, panic selling, or collective irrational exuberance.

The advent of the digital era has significantly accelerated the transmission speed of this neural network but has also introduced new risks. When the speed of information diffusion far exceeds the speed of institutional or structural adjustment, localized emotional disturbances may rapidly propagate throughout the system before regulatory mechanisms can respond, producing collective reactions similar to neural overexcitation. Phenomena described in behavioral finance—such as herd effects and information cascades—can be interpreted as typical coordination failures within such high-speed neural networks.

From the perspective of failure modes, this structure displays a clear similarity to biological nervous systems: when signal transmission becomes distorted or excessively amplified, the organism exhibits uncoordinated responses—localized stimuli may be amplified into systemic spasms (market panic), while the suppression of signals may produce sensory numbness (the disappearance of information under price controls).

It should be noted that this distributed neural network does not constitute the entire nervous system. In biological organisms, a large portion of daily metabolic activity is regulated automatically by the autonomic nervous system, which maintains basic coordination among organs through local signals. At higher levels, however, the central nervous system integrates information and guides overall behavior.

A similar hierarchical structure exists within the economic organism. The market signal system formed by the price mechanism and information networks can be understood as a distributed autonomic neural network. Most of the time, it coordinates economic activities automatically through local signals, enabling production and consumption to achieve basic order without central command.

At the same time, higher-level coordinating structures also exist within economic systems. In modern national economies, this role is primarily performed by government and its policy system. Through institutional rules, macroeconomic policies, and crisis-response mechanisms, governments exert directional influence over the overall functioning of the economic organism and, when necessary, regulate or adjust the market neural network.

In other words, the market neural network formed by price mechanisms resembles a distributed and spontaneously operating nervous system, while government plays a role analogous to the central nervous system. It does not replace the market neural network in conducting daily signal transmission, but it can integrate information at a higher level, establish rules, and issue policy signals that coordinate the system as a whole in exceptional circumstances.

Because the role of government as the central nervous system in economic metabolism involves institutional structures, policy signaling mechanisms, and its relationship with the immune system, its full theoretical discussion will be developed in Chapter 10. The anatomical framework in the present chapter therefore focuses primarily on the distributed signal systems upon which everyday metabolic operations depend—namely, the economic neural network formed by price and information mechanisms.

3.4.2 Circulatory and Endocrine Systems: Money, the Financial System, and Macroeconomic Policy

At this point, an important conceptual clarification must be made within the analytical framework, as it affects the accuracy of all subsequent metabolic analysis.

Money performs two functions within the economic organism, corresponding to two distinct but interdependent physiological systems: the circulatory system and the endocrine system. Clarifying this distinction is essential for understanding the mechanisms through which monetary systems fail.

Money is the blood of the economic organism—the medium of the circulatory system.

The core function of blood is transportation: delivering oxygen and nutrients from supply organs (the lungs and digestive system) to tissues and cells, while simultaneously carrying metabolic waste from tissues to excretory organs. Blood itself does not produce oxygen nor does it instruct cells how to behave; it simply serves as the carrier of metabolic substances.

Money corresponds precisely to blood in this sense: it is the medium through which value (negentropy) circulates within the economic system. Money itself does not create value; rather, it allows already created value to circulate among different metabolic actors. Firms use it to purchase factors of production, households use it to purchase goods, and capital uses it to seek investment opportunities. When monetary circulation stops, it resembles the cessation of blood circulation: the capital supply to all industrial organizations is immediately cut off and metabolic activity collapses. The sudden freezing of credit markets during the 2008 financial crisis represents a typical case of such a “circulatory arrest”: the blood (money) itself did not disappear, but the circulation channels suddenly became blocked, producing systemic liquidity depletion.

The financial system (including banks, securities markets, and insurance systems) constitutes the concrete network of circulation channels—arteries (major flows of funds), veins (return pathways of funds), and capillaries (microcredit networks reaching individual metabolic units) together form the complete circulatory circuit. A systemic collapse of the financial system is therefore equivalent to widespread blockage within the circulatory system.

Monetary policy functions as hormones transmitted through the bloodstream—the regulatory mechanism of the endocrine system.

This distinction provides the key to understanding macroeconomic policy. The endocrine system does not constitute a separate set of channels independent of the circulatory system; rather, it represents a regulatory signal layer that operates through it. Hormones are secreted by endocrine glands (such as the adrenal glands, thyroid gland, and pituitary gland), enter the bloodstream, circulate throughout the body, and bind with receptors on cells, thereby systematically altering cellular metabolic rhythms without requiring the precise one-to-one signaling characteristic of the nervous system.

The mechanism of monetary policy corresponds closely to this process. Central banks (endocrine glands) adjust interest rates (hormone concentration) or money supply (total hormone quantity), injecting regulatory signals into the monetary circulation system (bloodstream). These signals spread throughout the economy via monetary circulation, altering borrowing costs and investment expectations and thereby adjusting the metabolic rhythm of the entire economic system.

Low interest rates resemble elevated adrenaline levels: they increase metabolic activity and stimulate expansion in investment and consumption. High interest rates resemble reduced thyroid hormone levels: they lower metabolic speed and suppress overheated metabolic activity. A common feature of these regulatory processes is diffusion—hormones reach every cell through the bloodstream and cannot target only a specific industry or firm. This physiological analogy explains the “broad flood” characteristic often attributed to monetary policy: it regulates the metabolic rhythm of the entire organism rather than the function of any specific organ.

Large-scale expansion of money supply (quantitative easing) corresponds, in metabolic terms, to the injection of large volumes of blood into the circulatory system in order to prevent metabolic stagnation caused by liquidity depletion. Yet just as massive transfusions may dilute blood (reducing hemoglobin concentration), excessive money supply may produce signal dilution—that is, inflation. When the total volume of money exceeds the level that the economic system can effectively absorb and metabolize, price signals (the neural system) become submerged in monetary noise, and the transmission quality of ordered information deteriorates sharply. This represents a cross-system pathological effect in which excessive injection into the circulatory system interferes with the functioning of the neural system.

Fiscal policy, by contrast, resembles direct neural control over specific organs.

If monetary policy functions as hormone regulation diffused through the bloodstream, fiscal policy is closer to targeted neural commands directed toward specific effector organs. Through targeted public expenditure and taxation, governments directly inject or withdraw energy from specific industry organizations, particular regions, or specific cell groups (firms and households), rather than influencing the entire system diffusely as monetary policy does.

This distinction explains the differing effects of the two policy instruments and their respective applicability under different metabolic conditions. When the problem is system-wide metabolic stagnation (insufficient aggregate demand), the diffuse hormonal regulation of monetary policy is often the natural first choice. When the problem is metabolic disorder in specific tissues (such as the collapse of a particular industry or regional decline), the targeted regulation of fiscal policy is often more effective. Hormonal signals cannot distinguish between tissues that have already ceased metabolic activity and those that remain functional, whereas neural commands can target specific areas directly.

John Maynard Keynes's analysis of the liquidity trap acquires a precise physiological interpretation within this framework. When interest rates approach the zero lower bound, hormone concentration has already fallen to extremely low levels. Yet cells (firms and households), whose balance sheets have been damaged, refuse to respond to the hormonal signal—analogous to receptor failure at the cellular level, where additional hormone secretion cannot activate metabolic processes. In such circumstances, hormonal regulation (monetary policy) becomes ineffective, and targeted intervention through the neural system (fiscal policy) becomes necessary to restart metabolic activity in specific parts of the organism.

3.5 Nested Structures: Holons and the Historical Evolution of the Economic Organism

After completing the internal anatomy of the economic organism, a fundamental question must be addressed: at which level of life does the “economic organism” we refer to actually exist?

A firm can be an organism; a nation can also be an organism; the global economy itself appears to be an organism as well. This is not a contradiction but rather reflects the deeply nested structure of economic systems. However, one point is crucial when understanding this nesting: the relationships between different levels are not static relations of subordination. Instead, they evolve dynamically through historical processes. Higher-level super-organisms themselves emerge from the gradual coupling of independently operating lower-level organisms. Understanding this historical process of evolution is essential for interpreting the structure of the contemporary global economy.

3.5.1 Holons: Each Level as Both Whole and Part

The philosopher Arthur Koestler introduced the concept of the holon. In this universe, there are neither absolute wholes nor absolute parts. Every entity is a holon: it is a whole in relation to its internal components and a part in relation to the larger system to which it belongs.

Biological organisms perfectly embody this holonic logic. A cell is a complete unit of life, yet it is also a component of tissue. Tissue is an integration of cells, yet it is itself a component of an individual organism. Individual organisms form populations, and populations form ecosystems. Each level possesses a relatively autonomous operational logic while simultaneously being constrained by the environment of the higher-level system.

Economic systems display the same holonic structure. Firms are the most basic metabolic holons. National economies constitute mid-level holons, possessing relatively complete internal regulatory systems. The global economy represents the highest-level holon in the current historical period, within which nations perform specific metabolic functions.

However, the most crucial and often overlooked aspect of the holon framework is this: higher-level holons do not exist naturally; they emerge gradually through historical processes. To understand this, we must first examine the historical process through which economic organisms have coupled with one another.

3.5.2 From Independent Organisms to a Global Super-Organism: A History of Coupling

Before the opening of the Silk Road and before the Age of Discovery, most regional economies in the world functioned as highly independent metabolic organisms. Their production, consumption, resource circulation, and metabolic regulation were almost entirely completed within their own geographic and cultural boundaries.

The economic organism of Han Dynasty China formed a relatively complete metabolic loop consisting of agriculture, handicrafts, internal trade, and monetary circulation. Its internal price signals, institutional regulation, and cultural epigenetics evolved largely autonomously, maintaining almost no metabolic connection with the economies at the western end of Eurasia.

In the pre-Columbian Americas, the Maya, Aztec, and Inca civilizations each developed complete agricultural metabolic systems with their own equivalents of money, trade networks, and institutional regulatory mechanisms. There was no metabolic contact between these economic organisms and those of Eurasia.

In medieval Europe, feudal economies organized around the manor system created highly localized metabolic units. Individual feudal territories were nearly self-sufficient, and interregional trade occurred only at the margins.

Within the holon framework, these regional economies all functioned as highly independent individual organisms. They possessed their own cells (premodern forms of firms and households), their own tissues (proto-industrial sectors), their own regulatory systems (monetary equivalents, institutions, and cultural epigenetics), and their own boundaries.

The opening of the Silk Road represented the first major instance of cross-organism metabolic contact. The two ends of Eurasia became connected through trade in spices, silk, and precious metals. However, this connection remained sparse and high-friction. It resembled occasional exchanges of materials between two independent organisms rather than a systematic metabolic integration.

The Age of Discovery (15th–17th centuries) marked the first major leap in global economic coupling. The Atlantic trading network forcibly incorporated previously independent economic organisms in the Americas, Europe, and Africa into a shared metabolic network. Silver from the Americas flowed into Europe, reshaping the European monetary circulatory system. American crops—such as maize, potatoes, and tomatoes—transformed the basic metabolic structures of Eurasian societies. The forced transplantation of African labor enabled plantation economies in the Americas to become metabolic extensions of European capital accumulation. This was not a spontaneous coupling among equal organisms but a coercive metabolic integration characterized by profound power asymmetries. The integrated organisms lost part or all of their metabolic autonomy.

The Industrial Revolution and the wave of nineteenth-century globalization represented the second major leap. The expansion of the British industrial system increasingly integrated regions of the world as suppliers of raw materials and markets for manufactured goods. Previously independent economic systems—including India's textile industry, China's tea economy, and Latin American mining—were reorganized as peripheral components of the metabolic cycle of British industrial capitalism. By the late nineteenth century, the first true global super-organism had begun to take shape: nation-level organisms started to relinquish portions of their metabolic autonomy and became functional components within an emerging global division of labor.

The deepening globalization of the late twentieth century marked the third leap and the most deeply integrated stage of coupling to date. The Bretton Woods system established a unified framework for global monetary circulation. The WTO regime reduced the friction of material exchange across metabolic systems. Multinational corporations fragmented production chains and distributed them globally according to cost advantages. The integration of global financial markets allowed capital to move across national boundaries within seconds.

At this stage, national economic organisms have become deeply embedded in the metabolic network of the global super-organism. For many countries, core metabolic activities—such as energy supply, food production, technology acquisition, and capital flows—can no longer be completed within purely domestic metabolic cycles.

The analytical significance of this historical account is clear: the global super-organism is not a natural physiological fact but a contingent outcome of a historical process with direction, momentum, and the possibility of rupture. Its formation depends on specific conditions, its maintenance requires institutional costs, and its decoupling is possible under certain historical circumstances. Indeed, such decoupling has already occurred multiple times—for example, the dramatic retreat of globalization during the two World Wars represented a temporary rupture in the metabolic connections of the super-organism.

3.5.3 The Current Structure: An Asymmetrically Coupled Super-Organism

Once this historical evolution is understood, we can describe the current global economic structure more accurately without mistaking historically constructed power relations for the natural physiology of an organism.

The contemporary global economic super-organism is an asymmetrically coupled system. Different countries are integrated into the global metabolic network to varying degrees, in different ways, and at different positions. This heterogeneity is not determined purely by natural endowments but reflects the historical accumulation of power relations.

Some countries control critical nodes of the global metabolic system. The reserve currency status of the U.S. dollar grants the United States control over key channels of the global circulatory system. A small number of technology corporations and their host countries control critical nodes of the global informational neural network. Major oil-exporting countries control key switches in the global energy supply.

Yet these positions are not physiologically predetermined. They were historically formed through combinations of technological advantage, military power, institutional design, and geopolitical competition. They can also change under sufficiently profound historical transformations, just as the British pound ceded its reserve currency status to the dollar in the mid-twentieth century.

Other countries are deeply embedded in the global metabolic network but occupy relatively dependent positions. Their core metabolic activities rely on external energy, external technology, or external capital, and their internal regulatory capacity is strongly constrained by fluctuations in the global metabolic system.

This condition of dependence has long been the central concern of Dependency Theory. Within the holon framework, however, it receives a more precise description: such countries are not fully independent organisms but rather metabolic peripheries of the global super-organism. Their metabolic rhythms are determined more by the dynamics of the global system than by their own internal regulatory mechanisms.

3.5.4 Analytical Framework Across Three Levels

Within the analytical framework of this book, three principal levels are distinguished. Each level possesses its own analytical tools and policy implications.

Micro level: firms and households (cells).

This is the foundational level at which metabolic activity actually takes place. The objective of microeconomic policy is to maintain metabolic vitality at the cellular level: protecting property rights, reducing barriers to market entry, and ensuring the effective functioning of bankruptcy mechanisms (allowing unhealthy cells to be naturally eliminated without triggering systemic crises). The central pathologies at this level arise when the mechanism of cellular replacement is obstructed—such as the large-scale persistence of zombie firms—or when widespread metabolic stress emerges at the cellular level, as in household debt crises that lead to sharp contractions in consumption.

Meso level: national economies (complete organisms).

This is the most central level of analysis in this book. As a complete economic organism, a nation possesses a relatively autonomous metabolic cycle, independent regulatory systems (monetary policy, fiscal policy, and regulatory institutions), and identifiable boundaries (sovereignty, tariffs, and legal jurisdiction). The central task of macroeconomic policy is to maintain the overall metabolic health of the organism—preventing metabolic overheating (inflationary bubbles), metabolic stagnation (recessions and depressions), or the accumulation of metabolic waste (debt burdens and financial risks). Under conditions of deepening globalization, the “complete organism” character of the nation has been challenged—some metabolic functions have been partially transferred to the global super-organism. Nevertheless, national policy sovereignty still grants countries relatively independent metabolic regulatory capacity. This is why the national economy is chosen in this book as the fundamental unit of analysis.

Macro level: the global economic system (a historically formed super-organism).

When analyzing globalization, international trade, cross-border finance, and geoeconomic competition, the analytical framework must be elevated to this level. As discussed above, however, this super-organism is historically constructed and internally asymmetric. Its stability depends on specific institutional arrangements—such as the WTO, the IMF, and the dollar-based monetary system—and it has experienced significant periods of retreat and restructuring in history. When these institutional arrangements come under pressure, the metabolic connections of the super-organism may partially fracture, and nation-level organisms may seek to reinforce their own metabolic cycles. This dynamic provides a metabolic interpretation of the current wave of deglobalization.

3.5.5 Level Switching: The Key to Understanding Contemporary Tensions

The most important practical significance of holonic logic lies in its explanation of why national economic policy is often characterized by seemingly irreconcilable tensions.

The root of this tension lies in the dual identity of the nation. As a complete organism, it seeks to preserve the integrity and autonomy of its own metabolic system. Yet as a component of the global super-organism, it may voluntarily relinquish certain functions in order to improve overall efficiency and integrate more deeply into the global division of labor. The tension between these two identities is real and tends to intensify as global integration deepens.

During periods of expanding globalization, when the metabolic connections of the global super-organism are stable and the gains from integration continue to accumulate, being an efficient component of a larger organism becomes more advantageous than remaining a complete but less efficient independent organism. As a result, countries may voluntarily compress domestic agriculture (importing cheaper food), outsource manufacturing (to regions with lower costs), and liberalize capital accounts (to benefit from the efficiency of global capital markets). This represents a process in which an organism adjusts its internal structure in order to integrate into a higher-level metabolic network.

However, when the global super-organism itself becomes imbalanced or its connections begin to fracture, the instinct of the nation as an independent organism is reactivated. Trade protection, industrial reshoring, capital controls, and supply chain restructuring emerge under such conditions. These developments should not necessarily be interpreted as policy regression. Rather, they represent a physiological response of holonic systems: when instability is perceived in the higher-level metabolic network, the organism spontaneously adjusts toward safeguarding the integrity of its own metabolic cycle.

Once this dynamic is understood, many seemingly contradictory phenomena in the contemporary global economy acquire a unified explanation. Deglobalization can be interpreted as the adaptive response of holonic systems after excessive coupling with an unstable higher-level network. Technological decoupling between major powers represents a structural reconfiguration of metabolic networks within the super-organism. The reorganization of regional supply chains reflects attempts by mid-level organisms (groups of states) to establish new metabolic cycles when stability at the global level declines. This logic explains why regional economic blocs—such as the European Union or the RCEP—have accelerated internal integration under the pressures facing the global super-organism.

3.5.6 Three Criteria for Identifying an Independent Organism

When applying this framework, a practical question arises: under what conditions can an economic unit be considered a relatively independent organism rather than merely a functional component of a larger system?

This book proposes three criteria. They should be understood not as binary distinctions but as forming a continuum.

First, the degree of metabolic closure.

If most production, consumption, resource circulation, and capital redistribution within an economic system occur internally, and external shocks do not immediately trigger systemic collapse, the system can be said to possess a relatively independent metabolic cycle. The pre-Columbian Americas provide a typical example: agriculture, trade, social organization, and resource circulation were largely completed internally, with no metabolic connection to the Eurasian economic network. In this sense, they constituted a fully independent economic organism.

Second, boundary stability.

An independent organism must possess stable metabolic boundaries—whether these arise from geographic isolation, institutional barriers, extremely high trade costs, or limited information flows. The stability of boundaries determines whether a system can maintain its own metabolic rhythm rather than being dominated by fluctuations in external systems.

Third, the primacy of internal regulation.

If the price structure, resource allocation, and institutional evolution of an economy are determined primarily by internal mechanisms rather than by external markets or higher-level policy forces, the system possesses sufficient regulatory autonomy to be considered an independent organism.

The more complete the metabolic cycle, the more stable the boundaries, and the greater the priority of internal regulation, the stronger the attributes of an independent organism become. Conversely, the weaker these characteristics are, the more the system tends to function as a component of a higher-level super-organism.

By integrating all the structures discussed above, we obtain the complete anatomical framework of the economic organism.

This anatomical map is not merely a conceptual table of correspondences. Rather, it constitutes an analytical tool endowed with an internal diagnostic logic. It tells us that when economic problems arise, the first task is to determine at which level the pathology occurs and which system is affected.

Metabolic disorders at the cellular level (such as large-scale bankruptcies among firms) require interventions at the cellular level: protecting property rights, clearing exit channels, and reducing the frictions of regeneration. Metabolic disturbances at the level of cellular tissues (such as systemic crises within an industry or the obstruction of replacement mechanisms) require structural adjustments at the industry level, including directional industrial policy and the reduction of transitional frictions. Pathologies within regulatory systems (such as the collapse of the monetary system, institutional failure, or malfunction in the informational nervous system) require the reconstruction of basic regulatory functions; institutional repair often precedes economic recovery. Deep obstacles at the epigenetic level (fundamental mismatches between culture and institutions) must be recognized as belonging to a domain that cannot be transformed through policy directives alone. Changes at this level can only be advanced gradually through sustained institutional environments and the accumulation of historical processes across generations.

Problems at different levels require interventions at the corresponding levels in order to be effective. Attempting to solve epigenetic-level problems (deep cultural structures) through cellular-level operations (micro-level subsidies) resembles treating abnormal gene expression with topical medication. Such measures are not entirely ineffective, but they begin by targeting the wrong level of the problem and may even obscure deeper pathologies by creating the illusion of short-term improvement.

This chapter establishes the anatomical foundation of the “economic organism–metabolic theory.” In the chapters that follow, this anatomical map will serve as the reference framework for analyzing all economic phenomena: how this organism performs metabolism (Chapter 4), what forms of metabolic waste are generated in the process (Chapter 5), how money circulates within it (Chapter 6), and how crises erupt in physiological form when metabolism becomes severely disordered (Chapter 8).

Only by understanding the structure of the body can we truly comprehend how it functions—and how its illnesses emerge.
