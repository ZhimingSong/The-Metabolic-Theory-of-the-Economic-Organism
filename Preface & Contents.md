Preface
This book was written between late 2025 and early 2026.

Looking back from this point in time, one of the most significant moments in economics over the past two decades may have occurred in the aftermath of the 2008 financial crisis, when Queen Elizabeth II asked a question at the London School of Economics and Political Science: “Why did nobody see it coming?” A subsequent report submitted by the British Academy characterized this failure as a form of “collective failure of imagination.”

This answer may have been honest, yet it still leaves a deeper question unresolved. If a theoretical system that is widely regarded as capable of explaining market operations and guiding policy-making failed, for more than a decade, to identify the systemic risks accumulating within the system itself, then the problem cannot lie merely in predictive capacity. Rather, it lies in the very way in which we understand the economic system.

After 2008, global economic policy entered an unprecedented period of experimentation. Instruments such as zero interest rates, quantitative easing, large-scale fiscal stimulus, and macroprudential regulation played an important role in preventing the economy from collapsing into a crisis comparable to the Great Depression. Yet after these policies succeeded in stabilizing the financial system, the world economy gradually began to display a condition that is difficult to clearly describe using existing analytical frameworks: macroeconomic indicators returned to growth, but pressures within social structures continued to accumulate; technological innovation accelerated continuously, while the distribution of income and wealth became increasingly polarized; the prosperity of asset prices coexisted with stagnation in labor income.

In many economies, a generation of well-educated young people finds itself confronting a similar predicament: labor input continues to increase, yet prospects for the future become increasingly uncertain. Housing costs continue to rise, while the willingness to form families and engage in demographic reproduction steadily declines. The term “involution,” originating in a specific cultural context within internet discourse, has gradually become an intuitive expression describing this widespread condition. Existing economic theory is not entirely without explanations for these phenomena. Frameworks such as balance-sheet recession theory, skill-biased technological change, the distributive effects of monetary policy, and housing supply constraints are each capable of explaining certain aspects of the situation. However, most of these explanations remain confined to their respective analytical levels and have not formed a unified framework capable of simultaneously explaining macroeconomic structures, institutional evolution, and social consequences.

The question, therefore, is no longer simply why the crisis was not predicted. A more fundamental question must be raised: in what manner do we actually understand the economic system itself? This book attempts to address this question by proposing a different analytical perspective—one that understands the economic system as a complex organic system characterized by metabolic processes.

It should be clarified at the outset that the term “organism” here is not intended as a simple analogy to biological systems, but rather as an analytical framework grounded in functional isomorphism. Like living systems, economic systems must continuously acquire resources, transform energy, maintain structural organization, and sustain the continuity of systemic operation through internal regulatory mechanisms. From this perspective, economic activity is not merely a collection of production and exchange processes, but also a continuously operating process of social metabolism.

Within this framework, value creation can be understood as a process of negative entropy generation—that is, the capacity to generate structured order through the transformation of resources. Economic growth therefore manifests not only as the expansion of output, but also as an improvement in metabolic efficiency and structural complexity. Economic crises, accordingly, should not be understood solely as the result of exogenous shocks, but may instead reflect structural imbalances arising within the metabolic processes of the system itself.

In this sense, this book proposes a simplified metabolic expression as a starting point for analysis：

dVdt= A - C - S .

Here, V denotes the stock of value within the economic system; A represents anabolic processes (resource acquisition and value creation); C represents catabolic consumption required for maintaining structural organization; and S represents entropy losses caused by institutional frictions, information distortions, and the accumulation of waste. Economic growth corresponds to a state in which A persistently exceeds the sum of C and S, while crises may emerge when S rises rapidly or when A declines abruptly.

Within this framework, economic agents may be understood as fundamental units within the metabolic structure. Individuals, households, and firms constitute organizational layers analogous to “economic cells,” participating in the system's metabolism through production, exchange, and informational interaction. The financial system, in a certain sense, performs functions similar to a circulatory system, while the government is more closely analogous to a regulatory organ whose role is to regulate metabolic flows through institutions and policy, alleviate structural blockages, and facilitate metabolic reorganization when systemic imbalances arise.

At the global level, this book further proposes the concept of “economic trophic levels” to describe the hierarchical structure of the world economy. The positions of different countries and regions in terms of resource acquisition, technological capabilities, and value distribution resemble trophic relationships within ecological systems. This perspective attempts to explain why the global economy has long exhibited relatively stable structural differentiation, and why technological capabilities and industrial division of labor tend to form persistent hierarchical differences among nations.

It should be emphasized that the perspective proposed in this book does not seek to replace existing economic theories. Mechanistic models retain considerable value in many specific analytical contexts. The goal of this work is instead to provide a macro-structural perspective that complements traditional economics, particularly in areas concerning systemic evolution, structural complexity, and the analysis of long-term crises.

The book is organized into five parts. Beginning with paradigmatic foundations, structural anatomy, and metabolic physiology, the analysis gradually expands to include crisis mechanisms, institutional regulation, and the structure of the global economy. The first two parts establish the basic concepts and metabolic framework of the theory; the third part discusses measurement systems and historical cases; the fourth part applies the metabolic perspective to issues of culture, institutions, and demographic reproduction; and the fifth part extends the analysis to the global economic system, proposing a structural interpretation centered on the concept of economic trophic levels.

By introducing the concept of metabolism, this book attempts to construct an interdisciplinary analytical framework through which the structural maintenance, energy transformation, and cyclical fluctuations of economic systems may be reinterpreted. The author does not expect that this framework will provide definitive answers to all questions, but hopes that it may offer a new research pathway for understanding several key issues within the structure of the contemporary economy.
