Part I: Viewing the Economy Through a Different Lens

The Metabolic Theory of the Economic Organism：Chapter 1: Is the Economy a Machine or a Living Organism?

Abstract

This paper is Chapter 1 of the book The Metabolic Theory of the Economic Organism: From the Mechanistic Paradigm to the Living Systems. The complete manuscript of the book is available on SSRN (DOI: 10.2139/ssrn.6456938) and OSF Preprints (DOI: 10.17605/OSF.IO/KPXNY).

This chapter establishes the paradigmatic foundations of the Economic Organism–Metabolism Theory (EOMT) by diagnosing the structural limitations of the mechanistic equilibrium framework and constructing three foundational propositions of an alternative life-systems paradigm grounded in the Second Law of Thermodynamics.

The chapter's central argument is that the limitations of equilibrium economics reflect a problem of physical ontology rather than mathematical technique. Walras explicitly modeled general equilibrium as a mechanical balance system, adopting the conceptual apparatus of classical physics — reversible dynamics, conservation of energy, and the absence of irreversible waste generation. This corresponds physically to the First Law of Thermodynamics: a closed conservative system. The real economy, however, belongs to an entirely different physical category: an open dissipative structure, in the sense formalized by Prigogine, that maintains ordered complexity through continuous metabolic throughput and entropy export. The systematic inability of equilibrium models to anticipate endogenous crises, explain irreversible developmental transitions, or account for long-term systemic waste accumulation is therefore not a calibration failure but an ontological misplacement — the application of First Law concepts to a Second Law system.

The chapter evaluates four existing challenges to the mechanistic paradigm: evolutionary economics (Nelson and Winter), complexity economics (Arthur), Minsky's Financial Instability Hypothesis, and the institutionalist theory of path dependence (North). Each identifies a genuine limitation of equilibrium analysis — selection dynamics, emergent nonlinearity, endogenous instability, and historical irreversibility respectively — but none provides a unified physical foundation integrating these insights. EOMT supplies that foundation.

The chapter then establishes three foundational propositions and traces their translation into the analytical tools of subsequent chapters. Self-organization — the emergence of macroscopic order from local metabolic interactions without comprehensive design — establishes the metabolic process (anabolism A, catabolism C, entropy dissipation S) as the primary analytical object, providing the systemic premise of the core equation dV/dt = A − C − S. Homeostasis — the dynamic maintenance of key variables through continuous active regulation rather than static rest — establishes waste accumulation and excretory capacity as the central mechanism of systemic stability and disruption, grounding the metabolic waste theory of Chapters 5 and 8. Evolution — irreversible structural transformation driven by the thermodynamic arrow of time — establishes metabolic path lock-in as the physical mechanism of persistent developmental inequality and institutional rigidity, grounding the theory of metabolic hierarchy and lock-in in Chapter 17.

The chapter concludes by defining the market as a dissipative structure and identifying three analytical directions through which EOMT develops this definition: the internal metabolic dynamics of a single economic organism (Chapters 2–13), the trophic stratification of the global metabolic system (Chapters 14–16), and the polarization of metabolic hierarchies through involution, hegemony, and lock-in (Chapter 17). The governing methodological stance is not the engineer's question of how to design an optimal system, but the physician's and ecologist's question of how to diagnose metabolic pathology and identify conditions under which recovery becomes possible.

Keywords: mechanistic paradigm; Second Law of Thermodynamics; dissipative structures; self-organization; homeostasis; evolutionary irreversibility; metabolic waste; equilibrium critique

1.1 The Twilight of Mechanism: When Precise Models Encounter the Real World

When we look back at the theoretical edifice of modern economics, it is difficult not to feel a sense of admiration for the grandeur and precision of its structure. From the moral-philosophical intuitions of Adam Smith, to the general equilibrium system developed by Léon Walras, and further to the highly formalized Dynamic Stochastic General Equilibrium (DSGE) models of contemporary macroeconomics, economists have constructed an extraordinarily complex yet internally coherent mathematical language. Through this language they have sought to capture stable governing principles within the immense complexity of human economic behavior.

Yet when these models confront the persistently turbulent and highly nonlinear realities of the twenty-first century, an unavoidable tension becomes apparent. Theoretical models have become increasingly sophisticated, but their capacity to explain real crises has not increased correspondingly. Phenomena such as financial crises, secular stagnation, structural imbalances, technological monopolies, and demographic collapse can often only be explained rhetorically after the fact, rather than anticipated or understood from within the system itself.

Following the outbreak of the 2008 Financial Crisis, Queen Elizabeth II visited the London School of Economics and Political Science and posed a simple yet disconcerting question to the assembled economists: “Why did nobody see it coming?” Confronted with this question, the economics profession largely fell silent. That silence represented perhaps the most honest acknowledgment of the limitations inherent in an entire theoretical framework.

This disconnect does not stem from errors in mathematical derivation, nor from technical problems in parameter calibration. Rather, it originates from a deeper premise that has long remained largely unexamined—the physical metaphor underlying modern economics itself is approaching the limits of its applicability.

It must first be emphasized that the reflections offered in this book on mechanistic economics do not deny its historical contributions. On the contrary, the remarkable achievements of modern economics were made possible precisely because it developed during a historical period in which the mechanistic worldview remained highly effective. In an era characterized by relatively stable structures, manageable rates of change, and technological progress that was broadly incremental, interpreting the economic system as a machine—one that could be analyzed, adjusted, and calibrated—was both natural and productive.

However, as that historical window gradually closes, the theoretical framework grounded in the metaphor of Isaac Newton's mechanics inevitably approaches its limits. The mechanistic paradigm is not “wrong”; rather, it has become historically outdated.

The framework proposed in this book begins from a different premise: the economy is not a machine but a living organism. It does not follow the reversible equilibrium logic of Newtonian mechanics; instead, it operates according to the metabolic logic described by the Second Law of Thermodynamics and by the life sciences. Before presenting this alternative framework in detail, however, it is necessary to examine several questions with intellectual honesty: Where did the mechanistic paradigm originate? At the level of physics, where exactly does its limitation lie? What attempts have already been made to move beyond it, and why have those attempts remained insufficient?

1.1.1 The Origins of Mechanism: A Successful Borrowing from Physics and Its Historical Limits

To understand why economics embraced the mechanistic paradigm so deeply, one must return to the intellectual context in which the discipline itself emerged. The theoretical foundations of modern economics were established almost simultaneously with the high point of classical physics. Newtonian mechanics depicted a universe composed of stable structures governed by deterministic laws. The determinism of Pierre-Simon Laplace extended this vision of certainty to its logical extreme: if all initial conditions were known, the future could in principle be calculated completely.

This fascination with determinism profoundly shaped the theoretical intuitions of early economists. In his influential work More Heat than Light (1989), Philip Mirowski incisively described this intellectual motivation as “physics envy.” The deep admiration economists held for the formal precision of physics led to the systematic borrowing of physical models at the methodological level.

This borrowing reached its most explicit and systematic expression in the work of Léon Walras. Walras did not merely employ mechanical metaphors inadvertently; he consciously constructed general equilibrium theory as an economic translation of mechanical equilibrium. In his 1874 work Elements of Pure Economics, he explicitly invoked mechanics as the analytical blueprint. The forces of supply and demand in the market interact in a manner analogous to forces within a mechanical system, eventually coming to rest at an equilibrium point.

In Walras's own formulation, the market tâtonnement process resembles a mechanical system searching for a point of minimum potential energy. Prices function as signal variables that guide the system toward equilibrium, performing a role structurally analogous to constraint forces within a mechanical system. This was not merely rhetorical language but a clear methodological stance: Walras believed that economics could—and indeed should—become a precise science possessing the same formal structure as mechanics.

The supply and demand curves developed by Alfred Marshall continued this physical analogy. Market forces were conceptualized as interacting forces that would eventually come to rest at a particular point. Prices were assigned the role of a regulator whose function was to pull the system back toward its equilibrium value, much as gravity draws a pendulum toward its lowest potential energy point. Although Walras and Marshall differed in theoretical style, they shared the same metaphysical core: the economic system possesses a natural equilibrium state; deviations from it are temporary, and return is inevitable. This idea represents a direct projection of the Newtonian worldview into the realm of economics.

Historically, this borrowing was both successful and legitimate. In the early stages of industrialization, economic systems exhibited relatively simple structural hierarchies. Capital accumulation followed stable trajectories, and technological progress largely took the form of incremental improvement. During that period, the economy indeed appeared to resemble a machine: inputs were introduced, outputs were generated, deviations were corrected, and order was maintained.

The mathematical formalization of economic theory—rendering propositions precise and deductions testable—possessed its own independent epistemological value. It was precisely this effort that elevated economics from a branch of moral philosophy into a scientific discipline with its own methodological standards.

Critiquing the mechanistic paradigm therefore does not mean denying this historical contribution. Rather, it means recognizing that its physical foundation ultimately rested on the wrong branch of physics. Walras borrowed from classical mechanics—a science concerned with how particles reach static equilibrium under external forces. The economic system, however, more closely resembles the dissipative structures studied in thermodynamics: systems that never settle into static equilibrium but instead maintain dynamic order far from equilibrium through the continuous consumption of energy. To analyze metabolism using the tools designed to study a pendulum inevitably produces a distorted anatomical diagram.

1.1.2 An Overlooked Problem in Physics: Mechanistic Economics Uses the Wrong Thermodynamics

Before turning to different schools of economic thought, it is necessary to raise a fundamental problem in physics. This issue constitutes the real reason why the mechanistic paradigm exhibits systematic failure when confronted with real-world phenomena, rather than merely reflecting the technical problem that “the models are not sufficiently refined.”

At the level of physical analogy, mechanistic economics corresponds to a worldview grounded in the First Law of Thermodynamics: energy is conserved; a system can reversibly transform between different states; there are no irreversible losses, no accumulation of waste, and no arrow of time. Within this physical image, economic activity appears as a reversible transformation of energy between different forms. Capital flows, price adjustments, deviations from equilibrium, and returns to equilibrium can all be described by equations as processes that are, in principle, reversible.

The real economic system, however, corresponds physically to the world described by the Second Law of Thermodynamics. Every real process generates an increase in entropy: it produces irreversible losses of order and the accumulation of waste. Production consumes resources and generates pollution; credit expansion creates liquidity while simultaneously accumulating repayment risk; technological innovation improves efficiency while rendering the capital stock associated with older technologies obsolete; and the increasing complexity of social organization enhances coordination capacity while accumulating institutional inertia. All of these are processes that are thermodynamically irreversible. The economic system is therefore an open dissipative system that continuously resists entropy increase, rather than a closed mechanical system oscillating reversibly around an equilibrium point.

The Dissipative Structure Theory developed by Nobel laureate Ilya Prigogine provides a crucial physical framework here. An open system operating far from thermodynamic equilibrium can maintain—or even increase—its internal order through the continuous inflow of energy and the export of waste. This principle constitutes the physical basis for the existence of living systems. Dissipative structures are not conservative systems that oscillate slightly around equilibrium; rather, they are open systems that maintain dynamic order through continuous metabolic flows. Once the inflow of energy is interrupted or the discharge of waste is obstructed, their internal order rapidly disintegrates.

The economic organism can be understood precisely as such a dissipative structure. Its “health” does not consist in equilibrium but in the continuous and unobstructed circulation of metabolic flows. Its “crisis” is not merely a deviation from equilibrium but a systemic breakdown that occurs when the accumulation of metabolic waste exceeds the system's capacity for elimination.

This thermodynamic distinction forms the core of the physical foundation of metabolic theory and provides the physical justification for adopting “metabolism” as the central analytical concept of this book. Metabolism is precisely the mechanism through which living systems maintain the state of dissipative structure: through anabolism—the intake of ordered structures from the external environment—and catabolism—the discharge of waste outward—the system counteracts the irreversible pressure of entropy increase and maintains a dynamic metabolic balance.

The fundamental problem of mechanistic economics, therefore, is not that its mathematics lacks precision, but that it draws upon the wrong branch of physics. It employs the conceptual tools of the First Law of Thermodynamics to analyze a system that is fundamentally governed by the Second Law of Thermodynamics. When Léon Walras introduced mechanical equilibrium into economic analysis, the physical model he adopted was a science concerned with the static equilibrium of particles under external forces. Yet the phenomena to which economic systems belong require a different physical model: the thermodynamics of dissipative structures, a science that studies how open systems maintain dynamic order through continuous exchanges of energy and waste. This is not a minor technical discrepancy but an ontological misplacement—much like attempting to predict the evolution of a living organism using equations designed to describe the motion of a simple pendulum. In such a case, failure is a physical inevitability rather than a technical mistake.

1.1.3 A Shared Assumption Beneath Theoretical Opposition: Different Schools Inherit the Same Physical Error

When we turn our attention to what appear to be intense theoretical disagreements within modern economics, an intriguing pattern emerges. Although the major schools often engage in sharp policy debates, they remain remarkably consistent at a deeper level: they all retain the same underlying physical metaphor. None fundamentally challenges the equilibrium ontology grounded in the metaphor of the First Law of Thermodynamics.

Neoclassical economics represents the most direct continuation of the mechanistic paradigm. Rational agents are treated like precisely specified particles that continuously adjust their behavior under marginal constraints. The equilibrium point functions as the system's “gravitational center,” and any deviation is regarded as temporary noise. In The Methodology of Positive Economics (1953), Milton Friedman explicitly compared economics to physics, arguing that the value of a theory lies in the accuracy of its predictions rather than in the realism of its assumptions. This methodological stance effectively transplanted the instrumentalist attitude of physics into economics without questioning whether the underlying physical metaphor itself was appropriate.

When asked why Dynamic Stochastic General Equilibrium (DSGE) models failed to anticipate the 2008 Financial Crisis, the standard neoclassical response has been that the crisis represented an “exogenous shock.” This response is itself an implicit acknowledgment of the model's limitation: phenomena that cannot be generated from within the system are simply classified as disturbances originating from outside it. In this way, the internal consistency of the equilibrium framework is preserved, but only at the cost of excluding the most important questions from the model's analytical domain.

Keynesian economics, by contrast, recognizes the possibility of market failure. In The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money (1936), John Maynard Keynes argued that insufficient effective demand could cause the economy to remain trapped in a state of persistent underemployment equilibrium. This insight constituted a significant internal challenge to the classical belief in automatic equilibrium restoration.

Even more importantly, Keynes's analysis of “animal spirits” touched upon a deeper issue: collective expectations are not merely deviations from equilibrium but constitutive elements of the equilibrium itself. In this sense, Keynes opened an important conceptual fissure within the mechanistic framework. Yet in the work of many of his followers, this fissure was largely closed again. In practical Keynesian policy, fiscal stimulus and monetary expansion often function as external forces or “lubricants” that help the system move from one equilibrium to another, rather than fundamentally restructuring the system's metabolic organization. The door that Keynes opened was, in many cases, quietly closed by those who followed him.

Monetarism and the neoclassical synthesis attempted to integrate Keynes's short-run analysis into the long-run equilibrium framework of neoclassical economics, producing the familiar compromise narrative: deviations may occur in the short run, but the system will inevitably return to equilibrium in the long run. From the perspective of metabolic theory, however, this compromise effectively suppresses the irreversibility implied by the Second Law of Thermodynamics. It assumes that any accumulation of metabolic waste will eventually be cleared automatically by market mechanisms in the “long run,” without analyzing the concrete relationship between the rate at which waste accumulates and the system's capacity to eliminate it.

Japan's prolonged economic stagnation since the 1990s offers a powerful empirical challenge to the promise that equilibrium will inevitably be restored in the long run. Over more than three decades, metabolic waste—in the form of nonperforming debt, excess capacity, and demographic aging—has accumulated at a rate that systematically exceeds the capacity of equilibrium-restoring mechanisms. In such circumstances, the “long run” can become far longer than anyone initially expected.

Among the major schools of economic thought, New Institutional Economics comes closest to the problem consciousness underlying metabolic theory. The work of Douglass North on path dependence and institutional evolution explicitly introduced the perspective of historical irreversibility. This represents a genuine step toward a worldview consistent with the Second Law of Thermodynamics, and it constitutes one of the most important epistemological contributions of the institutionalist tradition.

However, North did not fully articulate the thermodynamic mechanism underlying path dependence. A shift from one developmental trajectory to another requires more than a change in incentive structures; it also requires sufficient metabolic energy—an activation energy capable of overcoming the metabolic inertia embedded in existing institutional pathways. Here lies a key limitation of institutional economics: it contains important insights, but lacks a systematic physical foundation.

Across these four traditions, a shared assumption persists: the economic system can ultimately be analyzed as tending toward some form of equilibrium. This assumption has proved highly effective in explaining stability and short-term fluctuations, yet it systematically downplays another class of phenomena: irreversible structural evolution and the long-term accumulation of metabolic waste.

From this perspective, the long-standing debates among different schools of economics can be understood as occurring within the same epistemological cage defined by the First Law of Thermodynamics. What different schools argue about is how best to describe a system that they all assume to be fundamentally equilibrium-seeking. Yet few have seriously asked a more basic question: if the system does not in fact tend toward equilibrium, should the very premise of the debate be reconsidered?

This, perhaps, is the deeper reason why economic debates continue endlessly yet often fail to reach the core of the real problem. It is also why the point of departure for this book is not the modification of any single existing school, but the replacement of the shared physical foundation upon which they all implicitly rely.

1.1.4 Existing Internal Breakthroughs: What They Achieved and Where They Stopped

Dissatisfaction with the mechanistic framework is not first raised in this book. Several important attempts at theoretical breakthrough have already emerged within economics itself. Any serious alternative theory must engage with these efforts and clearly articulate its relationship to them. Metabolic theory is not constructed in an intellectual vacuum; it stands on the shoulders of these earlier breakthroughs.
Evolutionary economics.

In An Evolutionary Theory of Economic Change (1982), Richard Nelson and Sidney Winter explicitly borrowed the mechanisms of biological evolution—selection and inheritance—to construct a framework that departs from neoclassical equilibrium analysis. Firms’ routines were analogized to genes, while market competition was treated as a form of selection pressure. This remains one of the most systematic economic theories grounded in a life-system analogy.

The central contribution of this framework lies in introducing a selection mechanism into economic analysis, thereby fundamentally breaking with the neoclassical assumption that all firms are rational optimizers. It reveals how market competition promotes industrial evolution through the selective elimination of inefficient routines.

However, evolutionary economics largely understands evolution at the level of firm-level competition and selection. It resembles population ecology rather than systemic physiology. It can explain which firms survive, but it lacks a unified framework for describing how an entire economic system maintains, dissipates, and reconstructs its own order in metabolic terms.

Why does an entire national economy accumulate systemic “waste” that cannot be automatically cleared through market selection? Why do some industrial ecosystems enter a state of chronic metabolic suppression rather than being corrected by competitive pressures? These questions require a level of systemic physiology above firm-level competition, yet the population-ecology perspective of evolutionary economics provides few analytical tools for addressing them systematically.

Complexity economics.

Research by W. Brian Arthur on positive feedback and path dependence, along with the broader program of complexity economics promoted by the Santa Fe Institute, introduced nonlinear dynamics, emergence, adaptive agents, and network analysis into economic inquiry. These methodological tools are highly valuable and possess genuine explanatory power in describing phenomena such as technological lock-in, cascading financial crises, and the emergence of urban industrial clusters.

However, complexity economics is better understood as a methodological toolkit rather than a theory with a coherent ontological commitment. It can explain the conditions under which phase transitions occur, but it lacks a systematic diagnostic framework for the overall physiological logic of an economic system—why certain structures accumulate metabolic waste, or why certain systems fall into states of chronic metabolic suppression.

In this sense, complexity economics often describes symptoms but lacks a pathology.

Minsky's Financial Instability Hypothesis.

Among existing breakthroughs, Hyman Minsky comes closest to the perspective of metabolic theory. His central proposition—“stability itself is destabilizing”—can be translated into thermodynamic language as follows: the systemic accumulation of metabolic waste during periods of stability provides the material foundation for crisis-driven purges during periods of instability.

Minsky described how the financial organism gradually accumulates fragility during stable periods—through the progressive evolution from hedge finance to speculative finance and ultimately to Ponzi finance—until systemic collapse occurs once a critical threshold is crossed. This logic is structurally homologous to the metabolic process in which waste accumulation reaches a critical level and triggers systemic expulsion.

Yet Minsky's analysis remains confined to the endogenous instability of the financial system. It does not systematically extend the logic of waste accumulation to the cyclical dynamics of the real economy, industrial metabolic structures, the accumulation of cultural and institutional rigidities, or the trophic stratification of the global system. He provided the most important historical precedent for metabolic theory, but his framework remains partial rather than systemic.

The shared boundary of these breakthroughs.

Each of these approaches reveals a particular limitation of the mechanistic paradigm and offers valuable analytical tools within specific dimensions. However, they lack a unified physical foundation—a framework capable of integrating evolution (selection mechanisms), metabolism (system maintenance and waste accumulation), systemic stabilization (homeostasis and resilience), and structural crisis (systemic expulsion of metabolic waste) within a single explanatory architecture.

The objective of this book is precisely to provide such a framework, grounded in the Second Law of Thermodynamics, while fully acknowledging the contributions of these earlier breakthroughs.

The relationship between metabolic theory and these existing approaches can therefore be summarized as follows: it inherits their problem consciousness, provides a more unified physical foundation, and extends the analysis to dimensions that remain insufficiently explored—particularly the trophic structure of the global metabolic system and the polarization of metabolic hierarchy as a systemic source of contemporary economic inequality.

It is necessary to clarify the intended contribution of metabolic theory. This is primarily a conceptual unifying framework, rather than a new formal model that surpasses existing analytical tools in mathematical sophistication.

What metabolic theory offers is a shared analytical vocabulary and perspective—concepts such as metabolic waste, metabolic efficiency, trophic levels, and metabolic lock-in—that allow phenomena such as economic cycles, global inequality, cultural inertia, platform monopolies, and demographic crises to be analyzed within a single conceptual framework, rather than through seven mutually incompatible analytical languages.

This unity is conceptual rather than purely formal. Its scientific value must ultimately be demonstrated through empirical validation.

1.1.5 The Silence of the Mechanical Model: Two Questions It Cannot Answer

The fundamental difficulty faced by the mechanistic paradigm today does not lie in the fact that it “explains phenomena insufficiently precisely,” but rather in its systematic silence when confronted with two specific classes of questions. These two questions, notably, are precisely among the most important economic issues of the twenty-first century.

The first concerns the emergence of new structures and the irreversibility of history.

The transition from agrarian society to industrial society was not merely an expansion of output, but a species-level transformation in the mode of social organization. The rise of financialized capitalism was not a fluctuation around a mean, but a profound reconfiguration of the nature of the economic system itself. The emergence of the platform economy has further altered the modes of connection among microeconomic agents, generating entirely new metabolic forms.

When confronted with such phenomena, mechanical models typically classify them as “exogenous shocks.” This classification represents a theoretical concession: an implicit acknowledgement that the evolutionary process cannot be explained from within the system itself. More importantly, the mechanistic paradigm is unable to explain the irreversibility of these transformations. Why do industrialized cities not spontaneously revert to agrarian societies when populations decline? Why does financialization, once established, create deep metabolic path inertia? These forms of irreversibility are natural consequences under the Second Law of Thermodynamics, yet they remain inexplicable within the conceptual framework of the First Law.

The second concerns why the systemic accumulation of metabolic waste leads to nonlinear collapse.

Following the 2008 financial crisis, the concept of the “Minsky moment” entered mainstream policy discussions. Yet the explanatory power of this concept derives precisely from its description of a phenomenon that the mechanistic framework cannot endogenize: how fragility accumulated during periods of stability is suddenly released in a nonlinear fashion once a critical threshold is crossed.

Within the mechanical framework, crises can only appear as exogenous shocks. Within the metabolic framework, by contrast, crises are endogenous systemic purges that occur once accumulated waste reaches a critical threshold.

These two explanations represent more than a difference in wording; they imply fundamentally different understandings of the nature of crisis. The former suggests that crises are accidental and can be prevented through better risk management. The latter suggests that crises are periodic and unavoidable metabolic processes, and that suppressing them merely intensifies the next purge.

From this perspective, the 2008 financial crisis was not an exogenous disturbance but the inevitable purge of credit-generated metabolic waste accumulated throughout the 2000s. The quantitative easing policies implemented after 2009, viewed through the metabolic framework, functioned as an exogenous hormonal injection that suppressed metabolic purging, postponing the accumulation of waste to an even larger future threshold.

These two forms of theoretical silence find a unified explanation within the thermodynamic framework of metabolic theory. In a system governed by the Second Law of Thermodynamics, the accumulation of irreversible processes constitutes both the material basis for the emergence of new structures and the endogenous driving force behind systemic crises. These two phenomena are manifestations of the same physical logic operating across different temporal and spatial scales.

In Newton's universe, a pendulum does not evolve into another device simply because it has oscillated many times. Yet in the economic world, such “mutations” are not exceptions but the norm. If the economy is not a machine oscillating around equilibrium, but rather an organic system that grows, differentiates, accumulates waste, faces periodic purgative crises, and may undergo structural transitions under the constraints of the Second Law of Thermodynamics, then the challenge faced by existing theoretical toolkits is not merely one of repair—it is a paradigmatic problem.

It is precisely in this sense that this book attempts to shift the physical foundation of economic analysis from the First Law of Thermodynamics to the Second, and to move the central analytical concept from equilibrium to metabolism. This transition builds upon existing internal breakthroughs while providing a more unified and physically grounded explanatory framework.

Beginning with the next section, we will establish three foundational propositions of this framework and clarify the specific meaning of “metabolism” as the core analytical concept within them.

1.2 Foundational Propositions of the Life-System Paradigm: Self-Organization, Homeostasis, and Evolution

Having examined the limitations of the mechanistic paradigm, identified the boundaries of existing internal breakthroughs, and established a metabolic theoretical framework grounded in the Second Law of Thermodynamics, we can now formulate three foundational propositions for the life-system paradigm.

This book treats the economic system as an organism. This is neither a literary metaphor nor merely a heuristic analogy. Rather, it is an analytical premise with methodological constraints: when analyzing any economic phenomenon, we assume that the system follows the basic principles of living systems—dissipative structures, metabolic maintenance, and evolutionary irreversibility—rather than those of mechanical systems, characterized by conservative dynamics, reversible equilibria, and temporal symmetry.

The three foundational propositions of this book are self-organization, homeostasis, and evolution. It is necessary to clarify why the term foundational propositions is used instead of axioms. In a mathematical system, axioms imply starting points that require no proof and are not constrained by empirical evidence. By contrast, the three propositions proposed here are empirical claims about the behavioral regularities of economic systems. In principle, they could be questioned by particular kinds of counterexamples. The use of the term foundational propositions acknowledges this epistemological fact while also indicating that these propositions possess sufficiently strong empirical support and thermodynamic grounding to serve as stable starting points for the analytical framework of this book.

More importantly, these propositions are not three independent philosophical claims. Rather, they represent three manifestations of the Second Law of Thermodynamics within economic systems. Together they provide the systemic foundation for the metabolic equation introduced in the next chapter (dV/dt = A − C − S). The concluding subsection of Section 1.2 will explain how these propositions are concretely translated into the core analytical tools of metabolic theory.

1.2.1 Self-Organization: The Emergence of Order Rather Than Its Design

The first foundational proposition is that economic order arises primarily not from external comprehensive design but from the spontaneous emergence generated by the local interactions of numerous autonomous agents.

In mechanical systems, order exists prior to operation. Engineers first design the blueprint, and components are subsequently assembled to perform predetermined functions. In living systems, however, the relationship is reversed: order appears after interaction. No single cell possesses a blueprint of the entire organism; each merely responds to local signals, yet through long-term interaction organs, systems, and ultimately a complete organism emerge. This is not a miracle but a thermodynamic process of self-organization in dissipative structures. Within continuous energy flows, local fluctuations are amplified through positive feedback, eventually forming stable dissipative structures with macroscopic order.

Economic systems belong fundamentally to the latter category. Micro-level agents make decisions based only on their own circumstances, local information, and immediate incentives. Yet it is precisely through these dispersed, heterogeneous, and sometimes conflicting decisions that macroscopic order emerges. Global networks of division of labor, regional industrial clusters, price systems, and technological trajectories are not the result of any single actor's design, but rather the sediments of long-term metabolic interactions within the system.

An important potential counterexample must be addressed directly. History contains many consciously designed institutions—central banking systems, monetary standards, and property-rights regimes. Does the existence of such institutions demonstrate that economic order can indeed be designed?

The answer proposed in this book is that localized and limited institutional design is possible and has indeed occurred historically. However, localized design and the emergence of systemic order operate at different analytical levels and should not be conflated. A central bank is a deliberately designed institutional node, yet the effects it produces within a real economy depend on its metabolic interactions with the entire economic organism. The outcome of these interactions cannot be fully designed unilaterally.

At this point, it is necessary to clarify in advance the relationship between the self-organization proposition and the discussion of the “developmental state” presented in Chapter 14, in order to prevent later misunderstandings. The self-organization proposition rejects the notion of comprehensive blueprint-style design, not the possibility of precise metabolic management interventions.

The relative success of industrial policy in South Korea and Japan, as well as the systemic difficulties encountered by industrialization strategies in Latin America, reveal not a simple dichotomy between effective and ineffective government intervention. Instead, they point to a more precise distinction: successful developmental-state policies respect the endogenous evolutionary logic of the economic organism. They adopt intervention strategies appropriate to the system's metabolic stage and guide evolutionary trajectories by activating endogenous metabolic capacities, rather than attempting to replace endogenous metabolic processes through external design.

Within the framework of metabolic theory, the difference between such policies and those of a centrally planned economy becomes clear. The former represents precise metabolic management, analogous to the functions of a nervous or immune system, whereas the latter attempts to externally substitute the entire metabolic order, following the logic of an engineer's blueprint. Chapter 14 will provide a full analysis of this distinction. For present purposes, it suffices to note that the self-organization proposition does not constitute a philosophical rejection of government intervention; rather, it imposes precise constraints on the manner in which intervention can occur.

Accepting the proposition of self-organization requires abandoning a deeply rooted illusion: that the economic system can be arbitrarily disassembled, reassembled, and made to operate according to design. This does not deny the importance of rules and institutions. Instead, it emphasizes that any institutional intervention must take into account its compatibility with the overall metabolic processes of the system, rather than assuming that policy objectives and policy outcomes are automatically identical.

1.2.2 Homeostasis: Dynamic Maintenance Rather Than Static Equilibrium

The second foundational proposition revises the traditional economic concept of equilibrium—a revision that follows directly from the physical implications of the Second Law of Thermodynamics.

In mechanistic thinking, equilibrium is a state of rest: the system reaches a balance among interacting forces and then remains near that point. Within the Newtonian universe, such static equilibrium is a reasonable description of a final state. In a thermodynamic world governed by the Second Law, however, the situation is fundamentally different. For an open dissipative system, “rest” means the interruption of energy flows, which in turn implies the spontaneous decay of order. For a living system, this means death.

The genuine state of life is therefore a dynamic process in which entropy increase is continuously counteracted through the consumption of energy, allowing key variables to remain within a viable range. This insight lies at the core of Prigogine's theory of dissipative structures: the stability of life is not a static equilibrium, but a dynamic stability maintained through continuous metabolic flows.

The homeostasis of an economic system is precisely this form of dynamic maintenance. Indicators such as inflation, employment, and liquidity are not “fixed” at specific values; rather, they are constantly pulled back toward tolerable ranges through complex negative-feedback mechanisms. Price adjustments, inventory fluctuations, and the expansion and contraction of credit all form part of this homeostatic mechanism.

Fluctuations are therefore not the opposite of homeostasis, but a necessary condition for its maintenance. Just as the human body maintains a temperature of approximately 37°C not by keeping it perfectly constant, but by continuously adjusting to external disturbances, economic cycles, localized recessions, and structural adjustments often represent physiological processes through which the economic organism repairs itself after metabolic waste has accumulated to a certain level—rather than deviations from an equilibrium path caused by exogenous shocks.

The homeostasis proposition must also confront an important counterexample: history contains many economic collapses that never returned to any stable condition—the hyperinflation of the Weimar Republic, the disintegration of the Soviet economic system, and the monetary collapse of Zimbabwe. Do these cases demonstrate that “homeostasis” is merely a wishful assumption?

The metabolic framework offers a different interpretation. Homeostasis is not an unconditional property, but a conditional proposition dependent on the integrity of the metabolic system. Living organisms can also die: when the metabolic system suffers damage beyond its regulatory capacity, homeostatic mechanisms fail and the system disintegrates irreversibly.

Within this framework, the cases of Weimar Germany and the Soviet Union are precisely examples of metabolic collapse triggered by the accumulation of metabolic waste beyond a critical threshold. The problem is not that homeostatic mechanisms do not exist, but that they can fail when the rate of metabolic waste accumulation becomes too rapid or when external metabolic inputs are disrupted.

From a metabolic perspective, the dissolution of the Soviet Union can be understood as the result of decades of accumulated institutional metabolic waste—including distorted information transmission, misaligned incentive structures, and a systematic decline in anabolic capacity—exceeding the system's capacity for metabolic discharge. It was therefore not a random deviation from equilibrium.

The central implication of the homeostasis proposition is not that “economies always return to stability.” Rather, it is that metabolic regulatory mechanisms exist, and their effective functioning is a necessary condition for the continued existence of the system, while their failure is a symptom that the accumulation of metabolic waste has exceeded a critical threshold.

The goal of homeostasis is therefore not the elimination of fluctuations, but the prevention of key parameters from breaching survival thresholds. An economic system exhibiting zero volatility is not necessarily highly stable; it may instead indicate metabolic suppression. Much like an organism whose physiological responses have been suppressed by heavy sedation, such a system may appear calm on the surface while deeper metabolic imbalances are quietly accumulating.

1.2.3 Evolution: The Arrow of Time and Irreversibility

The third foundational proposition introduces a genuine arrow of time into economic analysis. This is the most direct macroscopic expression of the Second Law of Thermodynamics: entropy increases in one direction, and time is irreversible.

Mechanical systems are, in principle, reversible. Their equations of motion are symmetrical with respect to time; processes can theoretically be traced backward, and the structure of the system itself remains unchanged. The Second Law of Thermodynamics, however, explicitly prohibits such temporal symmetry at the macroscopic level. Once metabolic waste accumulates, once structures form, and once history becomes inscribed within the system, time moves forward irreversibly.

Living systems follow this direction completely. Cellular differentiation is irreversible; tissue development is irreversible; organismal aging is irreversible—unless, as illustrated by the analogy of induced pluripotent stem (iPS) cells discussed in Chapter 17, sufficiently strong external metabolic conditions are introduced to reorganize the metabolic structure and achieve metabolic dedifferentiation.

Economic systems exhibit the same property. Their present state depends not only on current conditions but, more profoundly, on their historical metabolic trajectories. Technological standards, institutional arrangements, industrial structures, and financial systems are all the cumulative outcomes of long-term metabolic selection. They are the material imprints left in systemic structures by metabolic activities occurring at specific historical moments.

Such accumulation generates metabolic path lock-in, causing economies to evolve along particular trajectories. Even when theoretically more efficient alternatives exist, transitions often prove difficult because switching requires overcoming the inertia embedded in the existing metabolic structure. It requires sufficient metabolic activation energy to break the inertia of the established path.

This proposition resonates deeply with Douglass North's theory of path dependence and W. Brian Arthur's research on positive-feedback lock-in. Metabolic theory, however, integrates these insights within a more explicitly physical framework. The irreversibility of evolution is not merely a sociological observation within institutional economics; it is a necessary constraint imposed on economic systems by the Second Law of Thermodynamics.

The physical mechanism underlying metabolic path lock-in lies in the accumulated metabolic capital embedded in existing structures—technological knowledge, institutional routines, infrastructure, and social trust. The stock value of this metabolic capital means that shifting metabolic pathways often entails real metabolic losses. In many cases these losses exceed the expected metabolic gains from transition, thereby sustaining path lock-in.

The proposition of evolution further implies that institutions such as central banks, capital markets, welfare systems, and digital platforms are all economic organs that emerge endogenously during particular historical metabolic stages. Their appearance follows the logic of differentiation under evolutionary metabolic pressures, rather than the logic of mechanical design.

Once such organs emerge, they become structural constraints on subsequent metabolic activities. They expand the possibilities of certain metabolic pathways while simultaneously closing off others.

Evolution therefore implies two fundamental facts: structures change, and those changes are irreversible. Any long-term economic analysis that neglects this principle will inevitably become distorted. In particular, policy proposals that assume the economy can be restored to some “ideal” prehistorical state fundamentally misunderstand the irreversible nature of economic evolution.

1.2.4 Summary: From the Three Propositions to Metabolic Analytical Tools — Bridging Philosophical Premises and the Analytical Framework

The purpose of establishing these three foundational propositions is not to derive policy conclusions directly, but rather to define the range and form of legitimate questions within this theoretical framework. However, there exists a necessary transformation that must be made explicit: how do these three propositions translate into the metabolic analytical tools used in the subsequent chapters?

Self-organization → the basic object of metabolic system analysis.

The macroscopic order of the economic organism is not designed but emerges spontaneously through metabolic processes. Consequently, the central object of metabolic analysis is the metabolic process itself—anabolism (A), catabolism (C), and entropy dissipation (S)—rather than an externally specified equilibrium state. This constitutes the systemic premise of the metabolic equation introduced in Chapter 2 (dV/dt = A − C − S). The equation describes the dynamic evolution of the organism's net metabolic value rather than the adjustment path of deviations from equilibrium.

Homeostasis → analysis of metabolic waste accumulation and metabolic resilience.

The homeostasis of the economic organism depends on the continuous discharge of metabolic waste. The fundamental mechanism of homeostatic disruption lies in the situation where the rate of metabolic waste accumulation exceeds the system's capacity for removal. This forms the systemic foundation for the theory of metabolic waste accumulation developed in Chapters 8 and 9. Economic cycles are therefore not random fluctuations along an equilibrium path but systemic purgative processes triggered when accumulated metabolic waste reaches a critical threshold. Analyses of metabolic waste accumulation—such as debt overhang, excess industrial capacity, asset bubbles, and institutional inertia—represent the theoretical elaboration of the homeostasis proposition at the level of concrete metabolic mechanisms.

Evolution → analysis of metabolic hierarchy and metabolic lock-in.

The metabolic expression of evolutionary irreversibility is metabolic path lock-in: existing metabolic structures accumulate metabolic capital, creating activation-energy thresholds that must be overcome for path switching. This provides the systemic premise for the theory of metabolic lock-in developed in Chapter 17. Phenomena such as involution (the reinforcement of path inertia within low-hierarchy metabolic systems), hegemony (the external reinforcement of low-level metabolic pathways by higher-level systems), and platform algorithmic lock-in (the institutional fixation of pathways in the era of digital metabolism) all represent different historical manifestations of evolutionary irreversibility within metabolic structures.

At a broader scale, the global trophic hierarchy analysis developed in Part IV of this book (Chapters 14–16)—including questions such as why some countries remain locked into low-trophic metabolic modes and why trophic transitions require specific metabolic conditions—constitutes the direct theoretical extension of the evolution proposition at the scale of the global metabolic system.

These three transformations ensure that the foundational propositions of this book are not merely philosophical statements at the beginning of the chapter but serve as the theoretical foundations of the analytical tools employed throughout the work. Beginning with the next chapter, readers will see how these propositions are progressively operationalized and empirically grounded in the theory of value (Chapter 2), metabolic mechanisms (Chapters 3–7), metabolic waste theory (Chapters 8–9), organ-system analysis (Chapters 4–10), and global trophic hierarchy analysis (Chapters 14–17).

The focus of inquiry must therefore shift: from “how to control the system” to “how to improve the system's metabolic conditions”; from “how to eliminate symptoms” to “how to strengthen regulatory capacity and identify genuine metabolic pathologies.”

This shift marks a transition in analytical perspective—from the mindset of the engineer to that of the physician and the ecologist. It must be emphasized, however, that this transformation does not imply a retreat from analytical rigor. Rather, it demands the establishment of standards of rigor that correspond to the living characteristics of the system under analysis: not the deterministic certainty of classical physics, but the evidential reasoning of medical diagnosis and the systemic description characteristic of ecological science.

1.3 The Metabolic Framework: The Market as a Dissipative Structure and the Three Analytical Directions of This Book

After establishing the three foundational propositions of the life paradigm and clarifying the transformation between these propositions and analytical tools, a further operationally crucial question must still be addressed: if the economic system is a living organism, what exactly is the market?

This question forms the final bridge between the three foundational propositions and the concrete analytical framework of metabolic theory.

Evolutionary economics and complexity economics have already demonstrated systematically that the market is not a designed machine but a naturally evolving ecosystem. This insight constitutes the starting point of metabolic theory rather than its conclusion. On this basis, metabolic theory introduces a further level of precision: the market is a dissipative structure—an open system that maintains order through continuous metabolic flows (anabolism and catabolism) under the constraint of the Second Law of Thermodynamics.

This is not merely a rhetorical substitution but an analytical refinement that gives the framework concrete physical meaning. The “health” and “pathology” of markets can therefore be diagnosed and measured through the quality of metabolic flows, the accumulation of metabolic waste, and the strength of metabolic resilience.

1.3.1 From Ecosystem to Dissipative Structure: The Precise Formulation of the Metabolic Framework

Defining the market as a “naturally evolving ecosystem” (a contribution of evolutionary economics) and defining it as a “dissipative structure” (the further refinement proposed by metabolic theory) leads to three important analytical differences.

First, the analytical focus shifts from “selection” to “metabolic flow.”

The central problem of ecosystem analysis is competition and selection: who adapts to the environment and who is eliminated. By contrast, the core question of dissipative-structure analysis concerns the quality and structure of metabolic flows: what the organism takes in, what it produces, what waste it accumulates, and whether this waste is effectively discharged. Both perspectives are valid, but the metabolic perspective is better suited for analyzing the overall health of the system, rather than the competitive strategies of individual participants.

Second, the mechanism of waste accumulation gains a physical foundation.

The ecosystem framework can describe instability but has difficulty explaining why systemic waste accumulation is inevitable—a necessary implication of the Second Law of Thermodynamics—rather than a contingent phenomenon that could be avoided through better-designed market institutions. The dissipative-structure framework clarifies this point: every real metabolic process produces waste; waste accumulation is a physical necessity rather than the consequence of imperfect institutions.

This insight has profound implications for economic policy: the goal of policy is not to eliminate waste accumulation but to manage the rate of accumulation and ensure the effective functioning of excretory mechanisms.

Third, diagnostic standards for metabolic health can be introduced.

In the ecosystem framework, the primary analytical units are species diversity and competitive structure. The dissipative-structure framework allows the introduction of a richer set of indicators for diagnosing systemic health: metabolic efficiency (η)—the effective anabolic output per unit of energy input; metabolic integrity—the degree of autonomous closure within the internal metabolic cycle; metabolic vitality—the evolutionary direction of the current metabolic rate; and metabolic waste stock—the accumulated volume of unexpelled waste within the system.

These four dimensions form the analytical basis for the three-dimensional metabolic map developed in Chapters 14–16 of this book.

The core implication of understanding the market as a dissipative structure is therefore the following: the stability and prosperity of markets depend on continuous metabolic flows—continuous resource intake (anabolism A), continuous waste discharge (catabolism C), and the unavoidable entropy dissipation (S).

When the dynamic balance among these three elements is disrupted—when metabolic flows are interrupted, when waste accumulation exceeds the system's removal capacity, or when entropy dissipation surpasses the compensatory capacity of anabolism—the order of the market begins to irreversibly disintegrate.

This dynamic balance forms the systemic foundation of the core equation that will be established in Chapter 2: dV/dt = A - C - S .

1.3.2 The Three Analytical Directions of the Metabolic Framework: A Preview of the Book's Theoretical Landscape

Having established the central proposition that the market is a dissipative structure, the metabolic framework develops the analysis of this book along three principal directions. Presenting this map in advance allows readers to understand where each chapter is situated within the overall structure as they proceed through the text.

Direction One: The Internal Metabolic Dynamics of the Economic Organism (Chapters 2–13).

This direction provides a systematic analysis of the internal operating mechanisms of a single economic organism within the metabolic framework. The central question is: how does the economic organism create value through metabolic processes? How do its different “organs”—money, finance, government, culture, institutions, and population—perform distinct functions of anabolism, catabolism, metabolic regulation, and metabolic waste disposal? How does metabolic waste accumulate, and how does it trigger periodic purgative crises once critical thresholds are reached? In what ways do cultural metabolic memory, regime–metabolism compatibility, and demographic metabolic reproduction function as slow variables in the organism's evolution, shaping its long-term metabolic trajectory?

Direction Two: Trophic Stratification in the Global Metabolic System (Chapters 14–16).

This direction presents a macro-ecological analysis of the global economic system and constitutes one of the most original theoretical contributions of this book. The central argument is that the global economy is not a parallel aggregation of independent national economies but rather a super-organism structured by trophic stratification. Negative entropy flows systematically from lower trophic levels (“entropy feeders,” providers of standardized labor and resources) toward higher trophic levels (“negentropy feeders,” exporters of technology-intensive knowledge services).

Different economic organisms occupy distinct positions along three dimensions—trophic level, metabolic integrity, and metabolic vitality—and their developmental trajectories are determined by the combined configuration of these dimensions. What metabolic conditions are required for trophic-level transitions? What are the respective metabolic strategies of large countries functioning as complete organisms versus small countries functioning as specialized organs within the global metabolic system? What pathological mechanisms underlie metabolic aging in Europe and developmental stagnation in Africa? These questions lie largely outside the analytical reach of the mechanistic framework but acquire systematic analytical tools within the trophic-metabolic perspective.

Direction Three: Polarization of Metabolic Hierarchies—Involution, Hegemony, and Lock-in Mechanisms (Chapter 17).

This direction provides a unified analytical framework for some of the most important contemporary questions in micro-level political economy. The central insight is that involution (the autophagic dynamics of low-hierarchy metabolism) and hegemony (the systemic extraction of metabolic resources by higher-hierarchy systems) are not independent phenomena but rather two inseparable poles of the same metabolic hierarchy. The existence of hegemony forms the structural precondition for involution, while the persistence of involution provides the material foundation through which hegemonic structures can be sustained.

Metabolic lock-in mechanisms, embodied in asymmetrical institutional arrangements governing the ownership of metabolic capital, stabilize and reproduce this symbiotic relationship over time. The platform economy represents the most significant contemporary industrial form in which these three dynamics—involution, hegemony, and lock-in—are simultaneously embodied within a single closed system.

Together, these three analytical directions constitute the full theoretical landscape of metabolic theory as a unified analytical framework: from the internal physiology of a single organism, to the global ecology among organisms, and finally to the hierarchical structures of metabolism that operate simultaneously across macro and micro scales.

This conceptual map is the most important gift offered at the conclusion of Chapter One: before opening Chapter Two, readers are already able to glimpse the destination toward which the entire book is moving.

1.3.3 Methodological Statement: How the Metabolic Analytical Framework Studies the Economy and How It Differs from Existing Approaches

On the basis of the metabolic framework outlined above, this book adopts a methodological stance for studying the economy that corresponds to its thermodynamic nature. This is not merely rhetorical; it implies observable differences from standard mechanistic approaches along several specific dimensions.

First, in terms of the unit of analysis, the fundamental analytical unit of this book is not an equilibrium state but the metabolic process. The focus is not on whether an economy deviates from a theoretical equilibrium point, but on whether its metabolic flows remain continuous, whether metabolic efficiency is improving, whether metabolic waste is accumulating, and whether the system possesses sufficient metabolic regulatory resilience under external shocks.

Second, in the temporal dimension, this book explicitly distinguishes between short-term metabolic fluctuations and long-term metabolic evolution, and understands the current state of a system as the sedimentation of its historical metabolic pathways. This implies that the same policy instrument may produce entirely different effects at different stages of metabolic evolution; there are no universally applicable policy laws that transcend developmental stages. The zero-interest-rate policy implemented by central banks had a specific significance during the metabolic purgation crisis phase following 2009. During the later phase of metabolic recovery, after metabolic waste had already been substantially discharged, the meaning of the same policy becomes entirely different. Correctly identifying the metabolic stage is therefore a prerequisite for effective policy prescriptions.

Third, in the form of research questions, this book does not attempt to define any eternal “optimal market form,” nor does it propose institutional blueprints intended to solve economic problems once and for all. The form of inquiry will always remain the following: given a specific metabolic stage, historical pathway, and structural constraints, what changes in conditions can improve metabolic efficiency, reduce the rate of waste accumulation, and strengthen systemic resilience?

Fourth, in terms of policy objectives, the metabolic analytical framework reorders the priority of three economic goals. The first priority is metabolic resilience—the system's capacity to survive metabolic shocks. The second priority is metabolic efficiency—the anabolic output generated per unit of input. The third priority is metabolic growth rate—the current rate of economic expansion. Mechanistic frameworks often invert this order by placing growth as the primary objective. The cost of this inversion is the sacrifice of metabolic resilience—compressing the system's excretory capacity and allowing larger volumes of metabolic waste to accumulate—in exchange for short-term metabolic acceleration. This inversion represents one of the policy roots of the metabolic predicament widely faced by major economies in the post-2008 global era.

This research posture does not abandon analytical rigor; rather, it demands a form of rigor appropriate to the thermodynamic complexity of the object under analysis. The standard is not the deterministic certainty of classical physics, but the evidential reasoning of medical diagnosis and the systemic description characteristic of ecological science. A good metabolic analysis resembles a good medical diagnosis: it does not promise to eliminate every symptom, but it can accurately identify the underlying metabolic pathology, distinguish between excretory responses to metabolic waste and genuine systemic collapse, and on this basis provide treatments that correspond to the system's metabolic stage.
The three foundational propositions and the dissipative-structure framework of metabolism together constitute the methodological foundation of the “Economic Organism—Metabolic Theory” (EOMT). Beginning with the next chapter, we will gradually construct the core structure of the theory upon this foundation: the redefinition of value (the negentropy theory of value in Chapter 2), the operation of metabolic mechanisms (the organ-system analyses of Chapters 3–7), the mechanism of crises (the theory of metabolic waste in Chapters 8–9), the policy framework (the nervous-system analysis in Chapter 10), and finally the biological interpretation of global metabolic order (the trophic and metabolic hierarchy theories developed in Chapters 14–17).
